Abstract 

Reverse vaccinology has enabled sequence-based antigen discovery, but it over-looks the rich semantic knowledge embedded in the biomedical literature. Here we establish Semantic Vaccinology (SemVac), a paradigm that leverages large language models (LLMs) to predict protective antigens directly from scientific text. Benchmarking 14 state-of-the-art LLMs on a curated antigen dataset shows that text-reasoning-based approaches match or exceed specialized deep learning models in precision, while offering superior robustness on functionally ambiguous proteins. Intriguingly, explicit reasoning modes (e.g., chain-of-thought) increase recall but consistently reduce precision, revealing an over-reasoning pitfall in biological discovery tasks. Applied to the complete proteome of Mpox virus, SemVac recapitulates known protective antigens and identifies previously unrec-ognized candidates such as B20R, which our semantic analysis links to immune evasion and structural exposure. This work establishes literature-driven seman-tic reasoning as a powerful complement to conventional vaccinology, with broad implications for AI-aided scientific discovery.

Reverse vaccinology (RV) has fundamentally transformed vaccine development by enabling in silico screening of pathogen proteomes based on sequence features, struc-tural motifs, and genomic information [1]. Since its pioneering application to serogroup B Neisseria meningitidis, RV has facilitated the discovery of numerous vaccine candi-dates that would have been missed by conventional culture-based approaches [2, 3]. However, despite these advances, conventional RV overlooks a critical dimension: the functional and immunological knowledge accumulated in decades of biomedical liter-ature. A protein’s suitability as a vaccine target depends not only on features such as transmembrane helices, hydrophilicity, and subcellular localization, but also on its functional roles in pathogenesis, including adhesion, invasion, toxin secretion, immune evasion, and prior experimental evidence from heterologous systems [4]. Expert vacci-nologists routinely integrate such semantic information to evaluate antigen candidacy, a process of complex logical reasoning that has remained unautomated due to the lack of suitable computational frameworks. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) trained on vast scientific cor-pora offers an unprecedented opportunity to bridge this semantic gap. These models encode extensive biomedical knowledge and demonstrate increasingly sophisticated reasoning capabilities [5–8]. This raises a fundamental question: when provided with protein-related literature descriptions, can LLMs identify vaccine candidates as effec-tively, or more efficiently and accurately, than human experts? Here we introduce 

Semantic Vaccinology (SemVac) , a paradigm that uses LLMs to reason over literature-derived protein descriptions and output calibrated antigenicity probabil-ity scores (Fig. 1a). We systematically evaluated 14 general-purpose LLMs (both closed- and open-source) on a carefully curated benchmark dataset and validated the approach on the WHO-priority Mpox virus proteome, demonstrating that seman-tic reasoning can both complement and, in some respects, outperform conventional computational vaccinology. To evaluate the performance of LLM-based semantic reasoning in vaccine antigen discovery, we established a benchmark using a widely recognized third-party stan-dard dataset in the field of reverse vaccinology. This benchmark dataset consists of 246 bacterial antigens, including 131 protective and 115 non-protective antigens (Sup-plementary Fig. 1) [9]. Using literature snippets retrieved from PaperBLAST [10] as input, each protein query returning 268 publications in average from abstracts and full papers describing the protein or its homologs, we prompted each model to act as an expert immunologist and return a single floating-point value between 0 and 1 representing the protein’s probability of being a protective antigen. Given the substan-tial experimental costs associated with validating false-positive candidates in vaccine development, we adopted precision as the primary evaluation metric, recognizing that high-quality candidate prioritization is often more valuable than maximizing recall alone. Among the 14 evaluated models, closed-source state-of-the-art LLMs demonstrated superior predictive performance (Fig. 1b; detailed metrics in Supplementary Table 1). GPT-5.2 Pro achieved the highest precision (0.8974), with a well-calibrated score distribution and clear separation between positive and negative samples (Fig. 1c–e). Notably, models within the same family exhibited highly correlated predictions (Supplementary Fig. 3–4), suggesting consistent internal representations of antigenic features. Strikingly, the open-weight model Kimi K2 0905 reached a precision of 0.8382, out-performing state-of-the-art specialized protein language and geometric deep learning model PLGDL [11] (0.7985), which combines evolutionary-scale sequence modeling with structural reasoning. Other open-source models, including Qwen 3 Max (0.7887) and DeepSeek V3.2 (0.7500), also achieved promising performance. These results demonstrate that general-purpose LLMs, when equipped with structured literature evidence, can rival or surpass dedicated computational tools for antigen prediction, despite never being explicitly trained for this task. To verify that these predictions are not merely parroting explicit mentions of “vaccine”, “protective”, or “antigen” in the retrieved literature, we conducted a critical control experiment: we removed all papers containing such keywords from the input before feeding them to Kimi K2 0905. Performance metrics exhibited minimal decline (precision 0.8382 vs. 0.8358; Fig. 1g), indicating that the model makes comprehensive judgments by learning the intrinsic properties of antigens through extensive literature on protein characterization, rather than relying on superficial keyword matching. To guide practical deployment, we compared models along the axes of precision and inference cost (Fig. 1f). Closed-source models exhibited uniformly high costs, with GPT-5.2 Pro incurring 32.2 to 141.8 times higher expenses than open-source alterna-tives. By contrast, open-source models featured substantially lower costs, with even the most expensive open model (Kimi K2 0905) ranking 4th in precision among all 14 models. When assessed along the precision–cost axis, Kimi K2 achieves a favorable bal-ance between cost and precision (Supplementary Fig. 7–9). This positions open-source LLMs as practical, scalable tools for semantic vaccinology, particularly in academic and resource-limited settings where per-query budgets constrain experimental design. We extended our analysis to a more complex and diverse dataset comprising 600 positive and 600 negative samples spanning viral, bacterial, and eukaryotic antigens (Supplementary Fig. 2) [11]. Both GPT-5.2 Pro and Kimi K2 0905 maintained clearly separated score distributions between positive and negative groups, with comparable precision (0.9383 and 0.9327, respectively; Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 10), confirming that SemVac generalizes across pathogen types. Many LLMs offer an explicit “reasoning mode” (e.g., chain-of-thought, step-by-step deduction) intended to improve logical performance on complex tasks. We systemat-ically compared standard vs. reasoning-enabled inference across multiple models to assess whether such explicit reasoning benefits antigen discovery (Fig. 1h; Supple-mentary Table 3). The results were unequivocal and counterintuitive: while reasoning consistently increased recall (by up to 8.4%), and decreased precision (by up to 7.6%). This sensitivity–selectivity trade-off held across closed- and open-source LLMs. For example, Claude 4.5 Opus showed recall gains of 8.40% accompanied by precision losses of 0.63%; Kimi K2 0905 exhibited similar patterns. Why does explicit reasoning harm precision in biological discovery tasks? Notably, Kimi K2 0905, which does not rely on explicit long reasoning paths, achieved strong performance without the cost penalty, suggesting that implicit semantic abstraction is currently more effective than explicit deduction for tasks like antigen discovery. This finding does not negate the value of reasoning in biology but highlights that its form is critical: representation-level reasoning, encoded in model weights through pretraining, may better align with the nonlinear encoding of biological knowledge than explicit, language-based deduction. To assess real-world utility, we applied the SemVac workflow to the complete pro-teome of Mpox virus (Monkeypox virus strain Zaire-96-I-16; UniProt proteome ID: UP000101269; accessed on June 24, 2026; 187 protein entries), which has continued to spread in Africa and threatens global health security. Each protein was processed identically to benchmark samples, with Kimi K2 0905 generating antigenicity scores spanning a wide dynamic range (0.05–0.92; Fig. 2a). The top 15 candidates (Fig. 2b) were significantly enriched for proteins involved in host cell entry, membrane fusion, virion assembly, and immune modulation, biological functions consistently associated with protective immunity against orthopoxviruses.[13]. Among the top candidates prioritized by SemVac (Fig. 2c), four proteins—M1R, A35R, A30L, and G10R—overlapped with the predictions from PLGDL. Notably, M1R and A35R have been independently validated by wet-lab experiments as capa-ble of eliciting protective immune responses [14, 15] (Fig. 2c). In the case of A30L (MPXVgp140; ortholog of vaccinia A28L, a fusion complex component), its high prior-itization by SemVac was primarily driven by its critical function in membrane fusion. Interestingly, although A30L was identified independently of strong sequence homol-ogy, its concurrent prediction by PLGDL underscores how semantic reasoning can converge with structure-based algorithms to robustly cross-validate promising candi-dates. Furthermore, G10R has also been experimentally confirmed to induce specific immunity within the PLGDL study itself. Beyond these overlaps, the robustness of SemVac’s predictions is further substantiated by its successful identification of three additional well-known antigens—A29L, H3L, and L1R—within the top 15. These three targets have been extensively verified by previous wet-lab experiments to be highly immunogenic. Collectively, the recapture of these established and experimentally val-idated targets strongly confirms the reliability of LLM-based semantic reasoning in identifying viable antigen targets. The remaining eight top candidates represent newly prioritized targets that did not overlap with PLGDL predictions, indicating that SemVac captures immunological features often missed by traditional sequence- and structure-based algorithms. For example, B20R (MPXVgp181; ortholog of vaccinia C13L), a viral TNF receptor homolog implicated in immune evasion, received a high score (0.89). This work establishes that general LLMs can perform expert-like reasoning over biomedical literature to predict antigenicity, transcending the limitations of structure-and sequence-only models. This insight may inform the design of AI systems for drug target identification, protein function annotation, host–pathogen interaction analysis, and systems biology, all of which share core characteristics: limited labeled data, stringent biological constraints, and heavy reliance on unstructured literature. Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. Curated antigen datasets may contain biases and incomplete annotations, restricting the completeness of available evidence, while predicted candidates lack an integrated validation loop beyond static inference. Building on these limitations, future work may integrate LLMs into agent-based frameworks to enable iterative literature retrieval, hypothesis refinement, and downstream validation, forming a closed-loop system for accelerated vaccine target discovery. Overall, semantic vaccinology provides a scalable paradigm for AI-assisted biological discovery. 

## Method 

Dataset construction. 

A benchmark dataset of 246 bacterial antigens (131 protective, 115 non-protective) was curated from the published reverse vaccinology studies. Positive samples were defined as proteins with experimental validation of protective efficacy in animal mod-els; negative samples comprised non-protective bacterial proteins with no supporting experimental evidence from the literature to ensure the “true negative” dataset (Sup-plementary Fig. 1). An extended validation set of 1,200 proteins (600 positive, 600 negative) covering viral, bacterial, and eukaryotic antigens was assembled from Pro-tegen, IEDB, and published RV screens to assess cross-pathogen generalizability (Supplementary Fig. 2) [11]. 

Literature retrieval. 

To enable literature-driven antigen evaluation, we constructed structured semantic representations for each protein using the PaperBLAST [10]. For every query protein sequence, homologous proteins with associated publications were identified through sequence similarity search against the PaperBLAST database using protein–protein BLAST implemented in the NCBI BLAST+ software package. Matches with the E-value less than 0.001 were retained for literature aggregation. All linked publications corresponding to the query protein or its homologs were aggregated (268 publications in average for each protein; Supplementary Fig. 15). From these publications, text seg-ments describing each protein were extracted. The resulting textual corpus associated 7

Prompt formulation and model inference. 

To systematically evaluate the capacity of LLMs to identify antigen targets, a prompt framework was designed consisting of a system-level instruction and a protein-specific user input. The system instruction defined the model’s role as an expert compu-tational immunologist and vaccinologist, explicitly instructing it to integrate (i) literature evidence retrieved from PaperBLAST and (ii) general immunological prin-ciples and biological plausibility. For each protein, user input was provided in a structured JSON format that included protein identifiers, homology statistics (iden-tity and coverage), publication information and extracted literature snippets (an input example in Supplementary Fig. 14). This standardized representation ensured con-sistent input formatting across different models. All LLMs were accessed using the OpenAI-compatible SDK interface provided by OpenRouter, which provides unified API access to multiple frontier LLM providers. All LLMs were required to output a single floating-point value between 0.0 and 1.0, representing the predicted proba-bility that the queried protein is a protective vaccine antigen. No explanatory text or additional formatting was permitted, thereby ensuring strict comparability across models. 

Reasoning mode configuration 

To investigate the impact of explicit reasoning on antigen prediction performance, selected models were further evaluated using reasoning-enabled inference modes. Rea-soning mode was activated through the OpenRouter. For models supporting internal chain-of-thought or deliberative inference, reasoning was enabled through the think-ing parameter exposed by the API. This configuration allowed the model to internally allocate additional reasoning tokens before generating the final output. Apart from enabling reasoning, all other inference settings were kept identical to the standard evaluation protocol. The same prompts, input formatting, temperature settings (tem-perature = 0) and output constraints were maintained across both modes to ensure fair comparison. The model was still required to return only a single floating-point value between 0 and 1, representing the predicted probability that the queried protein is a protective antigen. 

Evaluated Models 

A diverse set of frontier LLMs was evaluated, encompassing both proprietary (closed-source) and open-weight models. To ensure reproducibility, all evaluations were conducted between January and March 2026. For all models, the decoding temper-ature was set to 0 to guarantee deterministic outputs. Unless otherwise specified, models were accessed via the OpenRouter using their official hosted endpoints, with identical prompt formatting and decoding configurations maintained across all models to enable fair comparison. 8

• Grok-4 and Grok-4.1 Fast [20, 21]: Developed by xAI, Grok-4 serves as a general-purpose frontier model, while Grok-4.1 Fast is evaluated for its high-throughput inference capabilities. 

• Qwen 3 Max [22]: A high-capacity model from Alibaba Cloud, designed for broad linguistic coverage and competitive reasoning benchmarks. Open-source and open-weighted LLMs include: 

• Kimi-K2-0905 [23]: An open-weight model from Moonshot AI, specifically opti-mized for long-context instruction following and reasoning-centric applications. 

• DeepSeek-V3.2 [24]: A widely adopted general-purpose model known for its highly efficient capability-to-cost profile and strong coding and mathematical reasoning capabilities. 

• MiniMax M2.1 [25]: An open-weight foundation model designed by MiniMax for broad language understanding and generation tasks. 

• Mistral Large 3 (25.12) [26]: The latest high-capability open-weight release from Mistral AI, serving as a primary research baseline for frontier-level open models. 

• Qwen 3.5 Plus [27]: A large-scale foundation model developed by Alibaba Cloud, designed for strong multilingual understanding and advanced reasoning performance across diverse tasks. 

• GLM-5 [28]: A frontier LLM from Zhipu AI focusing on enhanced reasoning ability and general intelligence across scientific and technical domains. For reference, we additionally report the performance of PLGDL, a specialized protein language and geometric deep learning model, as a non-LLM baseline. 

Evaluation metrics and quantitative assessment 

Predicted probabilities were compared against ground-truth labels to compute stan-dard binary classification metrics. Let T P , T N , F P , and F N denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. Given the substan-tial experimental cost associated with validating false-positive vaccine candidates, precision was treated as the primary evaluation metric in downstream compara-tive analyses. All probability thresholds used for confusion matrix visualization were selected consistently (0.5) across models to ensure fair comparison. 9
