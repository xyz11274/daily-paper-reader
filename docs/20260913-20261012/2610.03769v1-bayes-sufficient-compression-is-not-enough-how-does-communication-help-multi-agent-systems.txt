## Abstract 

Multi-agent LLM systems pair a sender with broad context and an executor with a limited local view. We study when a short message improves the executor’s next decision, when raw context is preferable, and when a stronger sender helps. Our framework, receiver-relative bounded coordination , expresses message utility as receiver gain minus protocol tax. Compression beats raw context when tax savings exceed losses from omitted information and decoder mismatch. Even 

Bayes-sufficient compression can fail when a bounded executor cannot use its surface form. A three-stage decomposition separates externalization, absorption, and action closure , explaining how errors remain after the correct content reaches the receiver. Under a single-crossing condition, sender upgrades help above a receiver-burden threshold. Across six benchmarks, the same Qwen protocol raises ContextBench joint accuracy from 0.633 to 0.775 but lowers ToolSandbox from 

0.889 to 0.653 . Fixed-message replay reveals closure failures despite correct artifact recovery. These results guide an inference-time selector that improves the accuracy-cost frontier on the evaluated communication regimes. Evaluation artifacts: https://github.com/Yydc/Communication .

## 1 Introduction 

Multi-agent LLM systems increasingly split work across agents with asymmetric contexts, capabilities, and budgets (Yi et al., 2025). In coding, web, and tool-use workflows, a main agent may inspect repository state, prior failures, tool traces, or verifier outputs (Dong et al., 2025). The executor, by contrast, must edit one file, choose one tool, or execute the next action under tighter context and latency limits (OpenAI, 2025; Srivastav and Gil Guzman, 2025; Anthropic, 2025b,a). This split makes communication a central protocol decision. Exposing the full context forces the executor to rediscover relevance within budget; a short message can expose the missing target directly, but may also omit useful information or present it in a form the executor cannot easily use (Shi et al., 2026). Recent evidence suggests that multi-agent LLM performance depends less on the number of agents than on how agents coordinate (Anthropic, 2025a). Matched-budget studies report coordination tax and topology-dependent error amplification; failure analyses trace breakdowns to specification gaps, inter-agent misalignment, and weak verification; production harnesses depend heavily on routing, aggregation, and context control (Kim et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2025b). The framework attributes this difference to the receiver’s bounded decoder and the protocol tax of the message (Section 2); Theorem 3 shows that Bayes sufficiency alone does not make a message receiver-optimal. observations raise a step-level question: when does a message improve the executor’s next decision rather than adding burden? Aggregate task metrics conflate these effects. A final failure may come from an uninformative message, a useful message the receiver fails to use, or an error carried across steps. Protocol design therefore needs to evaluate each message at the receiver’s next decision. We study this question through receiver-relative bounded coordination . The main agent, or sender, has a broader context and sends a bounded message to the executor, or receiver, which acts from a limited local view. Three commitments distinguish this framework from aggregate analyses: the step is the unit of analysis, the action-sufficient target rather than total information defines what is useful, and the executor is modeled as a bounded decoder, so an information-sufficient message need not be usable. Message value decomposes into the executor’s next-step gain minus the protocol tax induced. The framework connects four aspects of communication. Compression trades tax savings against omitted information and decoder mismatch. Bayes sufficiency preserves information, while receiver compatibility determines whether the executor can use it (Figure 1). Fixed-message replay separates content recovery from action closure , the conversion of recovered content into an emitted action. Finally, under single crossing, a stronger sender becomes useful above a receiver-burden threshold. We tested these relationships with paired receiver-only, full-context, and multi-agent runs; replay with a fixed sender message; and sender swaps. The six benchmarks cover function calling, stateful tools, code context, retrieval, software repair, and customer service. Communication helped when the local view lacked useful context, while its effect varied across receiver interfaces on locally informative tasks. These observations also guide a selector that chooses communication actions by their estimated gain, protocol burden, and cost.

## 2 A Theoretical Framework for Bounded Coordination 

We formalize bounded communication at the receiver’s next decision. The framework is receiver-relative : communication is useful only to the extent that it improves the deployed receiver’s next-step decision. The same quantities are extended to a discrete-action control rule in Section 3. Full proofs and theoretical supplements are in Appendix B.

2.2 One-Step Utility 

Fix a step and let δ ∈ { 0, 1} indicate whether the message is exposed: the receiver conditions on 

(Xjt , M t) when δ = 1 and on Xjt when δ = 0 . Let R⋆C (δ) be the lowest risk attainable by the bounded receiver class ΦC on target Z⋆t , and Rρ(δ) be the corresponding risk of the deployed protocol ρ.The receiver-relative gain A⋆C,ℓ (Mt; Z⋆t ) is the class-optimal risk reduction from seeing the message; for any other side information Yt, A⋆C,ℓ (Yt; Z⋆t ) is defined by replacing Mt with Yt. The protocol tax τρ,ℓ (Mt) is the increase in the gap between the deployed risk and the class-optimal risk after the message is exposed. It is signed: positive tax means the message moves the deployed protocol farther from the best decoder in the same bounded class. In multi-agent LLM systems, positive tax can reflect extra parsing burden, distraction from irrelevant content, and prompt-interface mismatch, or other protocol-induced errors. 2.3 Representation-Aware Compression 

Compression is useful only when the protocol tax it saves exceeds the information and usability costs it introduces. We separate two terms: omitted target information Iomit , the target-relevant information in raw context Ct but not recovered from (Xjt , M t); and decoder mismatch ΛC (Mt → Ct), the difference between how easily the bounded class ΦC can use the compressed message and how easily it can use raw context (positive ΛC favors raw; negative mismatch favors compression). Log-loss definitions are in Appendix B.1.

(2) Here ∆Ucmp −raw is the deployed utility of the compressed message minus that of the raw context, and ∆τraw →cmp is the tax saved by not exposing raw context. Thus, an action-sufficient message wins immediately when it reduces tax and is no harder for the executor to use than raw context. Appendix B.1 provides the proof, the expanded entropy form of Eq. (2), and an equivalent receiver-class certificate in terms of the representation gap BC (Mt, C t).

Bayes sufficiency is an information-level property, not a usability guarantee. In an LLM harness, the deployed executor is a fixed model with a fixed prompt, parser, context window, and tool interface, and the corresponding bounded receiver class is representation-sensitive : it is not closed under arbitrary recodings of the message surface. We therefore require compressed messages to preserve target-relevant information while using a strictly smaller budget than the raw context; the formal condition excludes degenerate copies and same-size recodings. 

Definition 2.2 (Proper Bayes-Sufficient Compression) . For 0 < α < 1, Mt = T (Ct) is an α-proper Bayes-sufficient compression of Ct for target Z⋆t and receiver view Xjt if (i) I(Z⋆t ; Ct | Xjt , M t) = 0 

and (ii) H(Mt) ≤ αH (Ct).

Even a proper Bayes-sufficient compression can fail when it is hard for the bounded executor to use. 

Theorem 3 (Bayes-Sufficient Compression Need Not Be Receiver-Optimal) . For any 0 < α < 1

and ε > 0, there exist a task, an α-proper Bayes-sufficient compression (Definition 2.2), and a representation-sensitive receiver class on which the raw context attains zero error, while the compressed message incurs an error of at least 1/2 − ε.

Concretely, a message may contain the correct artifact and action information, yet present it in a surface form that the executor fails to use. Appendix B.2 gives the construction, the finite-alphabet entropy version of the proper-compression condition, and the corresponding receiver-compatible sufficiency condition. It also shows that sufficiency becomes useful when tax savings exceed the receiver-class representation gap. 

2.4 Where Post-Message Loss Remains 

A useful message need not become a correct action. To localize this failure, we expose one internal bottleneck of the executor. A receiver is bottleneck-factorized if it first maps (Xjt , M t) to an internal target representation bZt, and then maps (Xjt , bZt) to the emitted action Ajt . Conditioned on the local view, this gives the chain Z⋆t → Ct → Mt → bZt → Ajt , where arrows denote conditional-independence structure rather than causal timing. 4Externalization is sender-side loss: useful content in Ct does not reach Mt. Absorption is receiver-side uptake loss: useful content in Mt does not enter bZt. Action closure is receiver-side decision loss: recovered content in bZt does not become the correct final action. 

Theorem 4 (Post-Message Loss Decomposes into Three Stages) . For any bottleneck-factorized receiver, 

Ltotal = Lext + Labs + Lclose . (3) 

Each term is nonnegative. The terms correspond respectively to externalization, absorption, and action closure. 

The theorem identifies where post-message loss remains, not merely its magnitude; mutual-information definitions are in Appendix B.3. This motivates the replay experiment: holding the sender message fixed across receivers removes sender-side variation and isolates receiver-side losses. 

Proposition 5 (Fixed-Message Replay Isolates Receiver-Side Loss) . For two receivers evaluated on the same local input, broader context, and the same message generated by a fixed sender and held fixed during replay, the sender-side externalization term cancels. The paired difference, therefore, depends only on receiver-side absorption and action-closure terms.

It may produce a more informative message, but it can also introduce additional protocol tax through longer messages, less compatible surface forms, or an increased reasoning burden for the executor. ## 3 Dynamic Coordination and Protocol Control 

We turn the step-level theory of Section 2 into an inference-time control rule that scores discrete protocol actions by the same gain, tax, mismatch, sender-upgrade, and residual-risk quantities. 

3.1 Residual Risk in Shared-State Workflows 

A one-step communication error does not always have the same cost. In loosely coupled tasks, later observations may resynchronize the agents. In shared-state tasks, however, an incorrect intermediate artifact, a stale plan state, or a workspace inconsistency may persist and affect subsequent steps. We represent this with a scalar Dt ≥ 0: the unresolved task-relevant discrepancy in the shared state. 5In our clipped residual model, let kt be the fresh discrepancy at step t, qt the resolved discrepancy, and λt the carry coefficient measuring how strongly residual persists through the shared state. For a counterfactual trajectory with residual eDt, the dynamics and residual gap are: 

Dt+1 = kt + λt[Dt − qt]+, ∆t := eDt − Dt. (4) The scalar notation is for readability; vector residuals follow componentwise. A residual gap multiplies forward while both trajectories remain in the carry regime; later observations or repairs can shrink the gap, and the gap vanishes only when the perturbed residual is fully resolved. ## 5 Conclusion 

Receiver-relative communication trades information loss and decoder compatibility against protocol tax and local burden. This step-level view explains why one protocol can help or hurt: compression pays off only when tax savings exceed omitted information and decoder mismatch, Bayes sufficiency does not guarantee receiver usability, post-message loss splits into externalization, absorption, and action closure, and a stronger sender helps only above a receiver-burden threshold. On six benchmarks, one fixed Qwen protocol lifts ContextBench joint accuracy from 0.633 to 0.775 while reducing ToolSandbox accuracy from 0.889 to 0.653 , and fixed-message replay exposes receivers that recover the correct artifact yet choose the wrong action. ## A Extended Related Work 

Harness engineering and multi-agent workflow design. A large body of recent work treats LLM-based multi-agent systems as an engineering problem of workflow decomposition, role allocation, and protocol design. Early systems introduced role-playing and conversational agent societies (Li et al., 2023), programmable multi-agent conversations (Wu et al., 2023), dynamic agent-group orchestration (Chen et al., 2023), standardized operating procedures for collaborative agents (Hong et al., 2023), and chat-based software-development teams (Qian et al., 2024). More recent software and agentic workflow settings instantiate explicit multi-agent or sub-agent structures to scale context handling, task decomposition, and execution, including self-organized coding agents for large codebases (Ishibashi and Nishimura, 2024), modularized workflow optimization (Niu et al., 2025), lightweight multi-supervisor orchestration (Sami et al., 2025), structured communication with hierarchical refinement (Wang et al., 2025), agile-role software engineering agents (Tawosi et al., 2025), protocol-driven multi-agent engineering (Mao et al., 2025), and agentic reasoning systems that orchestrate models with tools and refine solutions over multiple rounds (Zhou et al., 2025b). These works primarily propose better harnesses or workflow templates. Harness-aware evaluation makes the complementary point on the measurement side: reported reasoning performance depends on the harness around the 15 model as well as on the model itself (Zhou et al., 2026a). Our paper instead fixes the general sender– receiver setting and asks a different question: what information should move between components when the receiver is bounded? 

Training and equilibrium approaches to multi-agent coordination. A complementary line of work improves coordination by changing the agents or their interaction structure. Equilibrium-based methods cast multi-agent LLM reasoning as a Bayesian Nash equilibrium (Yi et al., 2025) or select stable coordination conventions through an entropy-regularized quantal response equilibrium (Xie et al., 2026c). Trust-region and sequential fine-tuning methods train LLM teams with per-update improvement guarantees and plug-and-play replacement of individual agents (Xie et al., 2026a,b), and in multi-agent reinforcement learning, acyclic reachability-based coordination reduces redundant communication between agents (Xie et al., 2025). These methods train or restructure the team, whereas our analysis holds the deployed sender and receiver fixed and asks when a single message improves the receiver’s next decision. 

Tool use, feedback, and agent-computer interfaces. Single-agent language-agent work also motivates our focus on next-step artifact/action decisions (Xiao et al., 2025, 2026d,c). ReAct interleaves reasoning and environment actions (Yao et al., 2023), Toolformer studies learned API-use decisions (Schick et al., 2023), Reflexion uses verbal feedback and memory to improve later actions (Shinn et al., 2023), and SWE-agent shows that the interaction interface itself can materially change software-agent performance (Yang et al., 2024). These systems emphasize that action spaces, tool schemas, feedback channels, and interface design shape what a model can turn into an action. Our receiver-relative view treats these concerns as part of the executor’s bounded decoder and protocol tax rather than as fixed properties of a benchmark. Work on single-model reasoning visualizes reasoning trajectories by their distances to candidate answers (Zhou et al., 2026c) and reweights policy optimization by rollout confidence and question difficulty (Zhou et al., 2026b); these methods study how one model reaches an answer, whereas our step-level diagnostics locate where a message fails between two models. 

Communication-centric surveys, protocol taxonomies, and failure analyses. A second line of work organizes the MA-LLM literature around communication itself. Beyond Self-Talk surveys LLM-based multi-agent systems from a communication-centric perspective (Yan et al., 2025), while A Survey of AI Agent Protocols analyzes the protocol layer and compares existing designs along dimensions such as scalability and latency (Yang et al., 2025). Most closely aligned with our motivation, MAST provides a fine-grained taxonomy of specification, misalignment, and verification failures across multiple MAS frameworks (Cemri et al., 2025). These papers clarify the design space and its failure modes but remain primarily descriptive or taxonomic. We recast these concerns as receiver-relative quantities for analysis, diagnosis, and protocol selection. 

Coordination benchmarks and protocol evaluation. Recent evaluation work has also made multi-agent coordination and protocol choice first-class benchmark targets. MultiAgentBench evaluates col-laboration and competition across diverse interactive scenarios (Zhu et al., 2025a); LLM-Coordination studies pure coordination settings involving environment comprehension, theory-of-mind reasoning, and joint planning (Agashe et al., 2025); and ProtocolBench argues that protocol choice should be evaluated not only by task success but also by latency, message overhead, and robustness (Du et al., 2025). Recent routers and resource-rational protocols, including MasRouter and auction-based communication, make related inference-time choices over collaboration modes, model assignments, or speaking rights (Yue et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025). Agent evaluations in other domains likewise separate surface success from task-relevant correctness, scoring reproducible speedups rather than functional patches in software performance optimization (Cui et al., 2026) and semantic faithfulness rather than compilation in natural-language-to-Lean formalization (Zhang et al., 2026). Our use of benchmarks is narrower and identification-oriented: RO / SF / MA contrasts, same-message replay, and sender swap serve here as tools for isolating communication gain, receiver-side loss, and alignment effects rather than as a broad leaderboard target. 

Communication under asymmetric information and protocol-sensitive collaboration. Commu-nication and Verification in LLM Agents explicitly studies collaboration under information asymmetry and unequal verification capability (Peng et al., 2025). This is closely related to our concern that the same message may be recoverable in principle yet unusable in a deployed protocol. Our paper 16 differs in two ways. First, we separate externalization, absorption, and action closure as distinct loci of coordination loss. Second, we tie these distinctions to same-message replay and sender-swap diagnostics, which enable us to localize receiver-side versus sender-side failures under a common step-level objective. 

Protocol choice as a control problem. Across harness engineering, protocol surveys, and protocol-evaluation work, one recurring practical theme is that communication choices trade off capability, latency, and message budget (Du et al., 2025; OpenAI, 2025; Anthropic, 2025a). Our closest connection to this theme is conceptual rather than architectural: we cast those choices as an inference-time control problem driven by receiver-relative gain, communication tax, and dynamic residual risk. The contribution is therefore not another routing architecture, but a common problem formulation that links modeling, diagnosis, and protocol selection for bounded receivers. 

Error propagation and recovery. Agent failure studies and safety frameworks also model how local errors spread through multi-step or multi-agent interaction (Xiao et al., 2026b,a). AgentDebug studies cascading failures through a modular error taxonomy and targeted feedback for recovery (Zhu et al., 2025b), while GUARDIAN uses temporal graph modeling to detect and mitigate hallucination and error propagation in multi-agent collaborations (Zhou et al., 2025a). Our conditional propagation law is complementary: it is a small analytic model for when a local residual gap carries forward or resynchronizes, rather than a full debugging or safety framework.

B.2 Bayes-Sufficient Compression and Receiver Compatibility 

This subsection proves Theorem 3. The result separates information sufficiency from receiver usability. A compressed message can preserve all target-relevant information for an unconstrained Bayes decoder while changing the representation geometry available to a bounded receiver class. Operationally, a deployed LLM executor induces a bounded receiver class through an interface tuple 

I = ( model , tokenizer , prompt template , schema/parser , tool validator , L, decoding policy ).

A compression is receiver-compatible for this interface when the required artifact/action fields can be extracted and converted into a valid emitted action with small excess risk. Under this view, field names, action verbs, file paths, and tool schemas are part of the communication channel rather than cosmetic formatting. For example, a direct payload such as {action_type : edit , target_artifact :

path } may be compatible with an edit executor, while {f1 : A2 , f2 : path } can preserve the Bayes target but fail under the deployed parser or policy. We use the following finite-alphabet proper-compression condition. A message M = T (C) is an

C.6 Protocol-Level Tax Knobs Calibration and execution. The early gate compares a receiver-sufficiency rate with a fixed threshold of 0.80 . The rate is the mean joint-hit indicator on held-out RO records for the deployed interface. The gate selects skip when the rate meets or exceeds the threshold, with its configuration fixed before testing. This path returns one receiver generation before any sender or scorer calls. After the gate, the five-action path generates and caches two real message candidates: compress 

summarizes upstream context under the message budget, and refresh summarizes the up-dated shared state. The raw condition appends full upstream context to the receiver observation. 

escalate reuses the compressed message and directly invokes the stronger receiver. Scoring uses five no-message and four conditioned probes, each capped at four tokens. Each probe pair uses the receiver that executes its action; conditioned probes use the actual candidate input. The gain surrogate clips the conditioned-minus-unconditioned leading-token log-probability difference at zero. The selected action reuses its cached candidate for one final receiver generation. Table 27 includes both candidate generations, all scoring probes, and the selected receiver call in each decision’s cost. 38 The default calibration and routing split assigns 20% of instances to calibration or router training and 80% to testing, grouped by instance_id . Repeated-split studies use 20 partitions with split seeds 0–19 and a fixed receiver, interface, and action set. Metrics are computed within each test split and averaged equally across the 20 splits. Selector decoding defaults to temperature 0. Paired calibration records provide an action-level protocol-burden offset. This offset can absorb gain effects as well as protocol burden, so the per-instance gain and cost terms supply the instance-specific ranking signal. The selector reads no benchmark label, gold target, hit field, or realized correctness at test time. The static score uses ηc = 10 −4 on raw token counts and ηr = 0 for its one-step horizon. The shared-state experiments evaluate residual propagation and targeted refresh separately.

## E Scope and Limitations 

Our account is step-level and protocol-relative: the framework makes a sharp prediction at this granularity and isolates the message-conditioning effect from trajectory-level confounders. Extending to end-to-end task success requires modeling cross-step error correlation; a natural extension would estimate fresh discrepancies and carry coefficients separately rather than folding them into one-step contrasts. The selector is analysis-guided; its gains are strongest when closure failure or protocol tax dominates, and richer online estimators, together with receiver-side adaptation, will extend its reach to other regimes. Paired MA, RO, and SF contrasts measure deployed protocol effects, and instance-clustered intervals quantify their uncertainty. The theoretical gain and tax depend additionally on the bounded decoder class; empirical calibration offsets combine these effects. Shared-state results hold when trajectory coupling is non-trivial; in loosely coupled settings, trajectories resynchronize regardless of intervention, a direct consequence of the dynamic framework. 

Broader impacts. Reducing unnecessary sender calls can lower inference cost and latency, making multi-agent assistance more accessible. The same communication mechanisms can spread incorrect directives or expose sensitive context to additional model services. Our observed target overrides and action-closure failures identify concrete failure modes for deployments. Applications should limit shared context to task-relevant information and validate consequential actions against the user’s request and available tool state. Lower communication cost also makes automated workflows easier to scale, including workflows used for harmful purposes; efficiency and task accuracy therefore need to be assessed alongside the intended use. 45 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
