# Understanding Conflict and Compatibility Conditions for Agents in AI-RAN 

Arshia Zolghadr †, Joao F. Santos †, Imtiaz Nasim ‡, Deniz Aytemiz ‡, Nicholas J. Kaminski ‡, and Jacek Kibiłda ††Commonwealth Cyber Initiative , Virginia Tech , USA, e-mail: {arshiaz,joaosantos,jkibilda }@vt.edu

Abstract —Disaggregated mobile networks expose open inter-faces for independent agents to control the Radio Access Network (RAN). The interactions between agents with contrasting objec-tives can result in conflicts. However, existing conflict mitigation methods treat agents that control the same parameter as being in a conflict that requires arbitration of their actions, without assessing whether their objectives can be satisfied simultaneously. In this paper, we introduce a control-theoretic approach to determine when agents can coexist and share control of the same parameters. We model threshold-based agents often encountered in the literature as sampled deadband feedback controllers and represent their objectives through Key Performance Indicator (KPI) targets and tolerances that define acceptable deadband bounds. We derive compatibility conditions from these bounds and show that compatibility for any number of agents and monitored KPIs can be established through pairwise tests. We propose a conflict mitigation policy that allows compatible agents to share control of the same network parameters while restricting arbitration to cases where their objectives cannot be satisfied simultaneously. We evaluate our policy through simulations using NIST’s ns3-oran extension of ns-3 , and our results show that it enables agents to share control with near-zero Service Level Agreement (SLA) drift when their objectives are compatible and the shared acceptable range is attainable, while matching Priority Mitigation when their objectives are incompatible. 

Index Terms —AI-RAN, Conflict Mitigation, Control Theory 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Mobile networks are increasingly adopting AI-driven op-timization of Radio Access Network (RAN) to achieve dif-ferent objectives. This evolution is prominently reflected in the AI-RAN vision, which has gained substantial momentum through the establishment of a global consortium with broad participation from industry stakeholders [1]. Such AI-driven optimization can be realized through a collection of third-party agents deployed on the network infrastructure, e.g., dApps, xApps, and rApps in the context of Open RAN (O-RAN) [2], that monitor Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and make decisions to modify RAN parameters. However, agents can make conflicting decisions with the potential to degrade performance, cause instability, or disrupt network operation [3]. The existing literature on conflicts in mobile networks focuses on conflict detection and conflict mitigation as dis-tinct problems. Conflict detection identifies interactions among agents, RAN parameters, and KPIs either based on agent sub-scription information or RAN data analysis [3]–[6]. Mitigation methods determine which actions take effect through different resolution mechanisms [7]–[9], assuming known or detected conflicts. While effective, this approach fails to recognize that not all detectable conflicts necessitate mitigation and that considering agents’ objectives can improve performance. For instance, in the case of a direct conflict, defined by O-RAN as two or more agents controlling the same RAN parameter [10], existing works typically treat any detected conflict as warrant-ing arbitration. However, consider a throughput-maximization agent and an energy-saving agent that both monitor downlink throughput and control the Physical Resource Block (PRB) al-location [3], [9]. The throughput-maximization agent requests more PRBs when the measured throughput falls below its target, while the energy-saving agent requests fewer PRBs when the throughput is above its target. Although both control the same parameter and are in direct conflict over it, if the throughput performance stays within both of their targets, this can satisfy both objectives, making arbitration unnecessary. Motivated by this observation, we focus on designing a miti-gation policy that accounts for agents’ objectives, enabling the agents to share control over parameters. To achieve this goal, we model a RAN agent as a sampled deadband feedback controller model [11], [12], reflecting the behavior of a typical RAN agent that monitors a given KPI and only takes action when its value falls outside its target objective [3]. Then, we show that a set of agents are compat-ible if there exists at least one network state, as characterized by their monitored KPIs, in which the objectives of all agents are simultaneously satisfied. On the contrary, a collection of agents is incompatible when no such network state exists, so at least one objective must be violated. This distinction allows us to propose a conflict mitigation policy that arbitrates only between incompatible RAN agents, thereby improving network performance and target performance compliance. The main contributions of this paper are as follows:  

> •

We provide the first control-theoretic formulation of AI-RAN agents and define their compatibility conditions.  

> •

We show that the compatibility of an arbitrary number of agents over an arbitrary number of KPIs can be established through pairwise tests.  

> •

We propose a compatibility-aware conflict mitigation policy that enables compatible agents to share control of a param-eter, while resorting to prioritizing a single agent only when their objectives are incompatible.  

> •

We quantify the impact of conflicts and compare state-of-the-art priority conflict mitigation policy [7] against our proposed method in a representative AI-RAN scenario using 1NIST’s ns3-oran extension of the ns-3 simulator [13]. II. R ELATED WORKS 

There is a growing literature exploring methods to detect conflicts based on the interactions between agents, RAN parameters, and KPIs. For example, the work of [3] adopted statistical profiles of agents in a sandbox environment to infer interactions between agents, parameters, and KPIs. In our prior works, we proposed data-driven methods to identify interactions based on the correlation [5] and similarity [6] between parameters and KPIs. While differing in their methods to identify interactions, existing works classify agents that control or influence the same parameter as being in conflict and require mitigation. This stringent approach overlooks cases in which multiple agents have compatible objectives and can share control or influence over a parameter without nega-tively affecting each other. In other words, existing conflict detection methods identify which agents interact on the same parameters, but do not determine whether mitigation is actually necessary. Another group of works explores methods to mitigate conflicts after they are detected. For example, distillation-based methods explore knowledge distillation to merge multi-ple Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)-based agents into a single aggregate agent trained to prevent conflicts [14]. However, this approach forfeits the modularity of indepen-dent agents, requires retraining, and can only be applied to DRL-based agents. Alternatively, prioritization-based methods (also referred to as rule-based in some papers) rank agents according to an arbitrary priority and only enact the actions of the highest-ranked agent [7]–[9], [15]. While these works adopt different prioritization strategies, they rely on the same mechanism: the actions of one agent take precedence, and others’ are suppressed, either permanently or at each decision epoch. This suppression is applied regardless of whether the other agents’ actions would conflict with the prioritized agent or contribute to achieving the same (or a similar) objective. The existing literature focuses on preventing potential ef-fects arising from multiple agents interacting over the same parameters, without assessing the compatibility of their ob-jectives. As a result, existing conflict mitigation methods can unnecessarily restrict the number of agents that can simulta-neously control the network infrastructure, potentially curbing the achievable performance. To address this limitation, we examine the behavior of agents operating on the network infrastructure and leverage control-theoretic concepts to derive the conditions under which multiple agents can coexist and share control of the same parameters. III. S YSTEM MODEL 

In the following, we present the RAN model and then define the agents as sampled deadband feedback controllers. We fo-cus on the Near Real-Time RAN Intelligent Controller (Near-RT RIC) because it is the RAN control domain in which xApps use E2 measurements and actions for near-real-time control and optimization of RAN elements and resources, and RAN conflict mitigation is defined for multi-xApp operation [10], [16]. However, the framework can potentially be extended to other control domains of AI-RAN.

B. Modeling the Operation of RAN Agents 

Agents monitor KPIs and act only when a monitored KPI leaves a range of values they consider satisfactory. For example, a throughput maximization agent only requests ad-ditional resources when the throughput falls below its intended threshold [3]. This behavior corresponds to a sampled dead-band feedback controller: the agent remains inactive while its monitored KPI lies within a range and acts to steer it back when it leaves that range [11], [12]. In this section, we evaluate our compatibility-aware conflict mitigation policy through simulations of a direct conflict over the downlink PRB allocation. We use the canonical two-agent, single-KPI case to isolate the effect of compatibility on arbitration and runtime behavior. We first describe the simulation and conflict scenario, then the metric and baselines, and finally the numerical results. 

A. Simulation Setup and Conflict Scenario 

To assess the compatibility of agents in AI-RAN and evalu-ate our conflict mitigation policy, we use NIST’s ns3-oran 

module [13], an extension of ns-3 that models O-RAN-like behavior and enables agents to monitor KPIs and control parameters of an LTE network. Our simulations consider a single-cell topology to isolate the effects of direct conflicts from those of inter-cell interference, handovers, and inter-cell coordination. We place stationary User Equipments (UEs) according to the 3GPP TR 38.901 [18] UMa deployment model, so changes in the reported throughput result from changes to the PRB allocation rather than mobility. We use constant-rate UDP downlink traffic so throughput changes reflect PRB control and cell capacity rather than transport-layer rate adaptation. Each run lasts 15 s with the 2 s used for initialization, and KPIs are evaluated over the remaining 13 s. C. Evaluating Compatibility Conditions 

In this analysis, we evaluate how differences between the agents’ objectives impact their ability to coexist while sharing the control of a parameter. Since their shared acceptable range is determined by their target values and tolerances, we set equal tolerances, δTM = δES = 10 Mbps , so that both agents have acceptable ranges of the same width, and vary the separation between their targets, ∆r. We consider 10 active UEs generating 4.5 Mbps of traffic each, and use 28 Mbps (an achievable throughput under this traffic demand) as the calibration point for the agents’ targets. We place the targets symmetrically around this reference value, with 

rTM = 28 + ∆ r/ 2 and rES = 28 − ∆r/ 2, yielding the shared acceptable throughput range [ 18 + ∆ r/ 2, 38 − ∆r/ 2 ] Mbps. Varying ∆r allows us to reduce the alignment between the agents’ objectives while keeping their targets centered around a feasible network state. By (15), its width W decreases linearly with ∆r and collapses at ∆r∗ = 2 δ = 20 Mbps .Figure 1 shows the results of our simulations. The Ad Hoc Shared Control maintains near-zero SLA drift while the agents are compatible and the shared acceptable range is attainable with the evaluated PRB allocations. As ∆r approaches ∆r∗,the shared acceptable range narrows until the available PRB allocations can no longer induce a throughput inside it, and the SLA drift increases even though the agents remain compatible. In contrast, the Priority Mitigation suppresses one agent’s actions at the cost of violating that agent’s objective, even when the agents are compatible. Our proposed policy displays three separate regimes accord-ing to ∆r. For ∆r ≤ 17 Mbps , the SLA drift remains below 

0.2% , indicating that the agents’ objectives are jointly satisfied. The throughput changes with ∆r, but it remains within the shared acceptable range. For 18 Mbps ≤ ∆r ≤ 20 Mbps ,the agents remain compatible, but none of the discrete PRB allocations induces a throughput within the narrowing shared acceptable range. This separates objective compatibility from practical attainability: the shared acceptable range remains non-empty, but no available PRB configuration realizes it. Consequently, SLA drift increases to ≈5.8% despite com-patibility, with no substantial throughput degradation. For 

∆r > ∆r∗, the shared acceptable range is empty and agents are incompatible. Only then does our policy perform arbitration, prioritizing the ES. The combined SLA drift grows with ∆r,reaching ≈26% at ∆r = 25 Mbps , as TM’s objective is no longer satisfied after its conflicting requests are suppressed. These results show that our compatibility test can identify conditions where agents are compatible and can share control of parameters without unnecessary arbitration.

Future work will relax the assumptions of the current formulation by incorporating an explicit RAN plant model to assess attainability alongside compatibility and to analyze the closed-loop behavior of incompatible agents under the deadband nonlinearity. We will also consider het-erogeneous sampling periods, coupled multi-KPI objectives, multiple shared control parameters, and indirect and implicit conflicts. Together, these directions would extend objective compatibility beyond the direct-conflict setting considered here toward more general coordination of heterogeneous agents in AI-RAN.
