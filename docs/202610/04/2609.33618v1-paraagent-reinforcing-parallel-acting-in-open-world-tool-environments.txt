# ParaAgent: Reinforcing Parallel Acting in Open-World Tool Environments 

Shengbin Yue 1, Hongru Wang 3, Siyuan Wang 4, Xiaoxin Chen 6, Wei Chen 5, Zhongyu Wei 1,21Fudan University, 2Shanghai Innovation Institute, 3University of Edinburgh, 4Chinese University of Hong Kong, 5Huazhong University of Science and Technology, 6Vivo AI Language model agents are increasingly deployed in open-world tool environments, which require balancing exploring unknown capabilities and exploiting known ones. Existing methods face a performance–efficiency tradeoff: they either rigidly decouple exploration and execution or interleave them without coordination. We argue that the key lies not in whether to decouple or interleave them, but in how to coordinate them across granularities. We introduce ParaAct , a structured parallel-action loop that combines phase-level Exploration ⇌ Execution with action-level parallelism. To learn this loop, ParaAgent combines multi-agent cold-start demonstrations with reinforcement learning under multi-level advantage decoupling, making planning structure explicit and supervising it with step-, phase-, and trajectory-level rewards. Learning is supported by our ToolEnv , a scalable simulator grounded in 50,011 realistic tool interfaces. On two open-world tool benchmarks, 

ParaAgent 4B achieves the best average success among all baselines, including GPT-4.1 systems, with the largest gains on multi-tool tasks. Behavioral analyses show that these gains stem from this action organization, highlighting its importance for capable and efficient open-world agents.

# 1 Introduction 

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) has accelerated the transition from chatbots toward general agentic assistants (Wei et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026; Yue et al., 2025b). Alongside this, the external tool ecosystem is rapidly expanding in scale and diversity, spanning APIs (Qin et al., 2024), MCP (Hou et al., 2025), and Skills (Zhang et al., 2025a). This is propelling LLM agents into open-world tool environments , where task constraints and tool utility often emerge progressively through interaction rather than specified in advance (Yao et al., 2023). Effective operation therefore requires balancing exploration of unfamiliar capabilities with exploitation of acquired information. Existing approaches broadly follow two paradigms, as shown in Figure 1. (1) Exploration-then-Execution (Li et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2024; Kachuee et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026) first explores a potential tool space, and then acts within this fixed context. Such decoupling enables tailored phase-wise optimization, facilitating more effective action strategies. However, early exploration errors or emergent requirements may remain unaddressed and ultimately limit task success. (2) Exploration-and-Execution can flexibly perform two types of actions, but existing methods (Qin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) naively interleave them as homogeneous actions in a flat sequence. Without explicit phase structure or coordination, this flexibility relies on frequent serial interleaving, accumulating irrelevant context over time. This exposes a clear performance-efficiency tradeoff : the former is efficient but brittle, whereas the latter is adaptive but interaction heavy. The key is not whether to separate or interleave exploration and execution, but how to coordinate them across granularities, phase-level adaptation with action-level parallelism . Two challenges arise in (i) Structure :exploration is epistemic , reducing uncertainty about required capabilities, whereas execution is operational ,coordinating under dependency. (ii) Learning : the loop relies on structural decisions that in-context or imitation learning can initialize, but robustly optimizing them requires interaction (Zhang et al., 2025b). It further requires rewarding how actions are organized, as such decisions are unobservable in individual actions, with effects emerging only phases later. To address (i) , we introduce ParaAct , a structured parallel-action loop framework that integrates verbal reasoning, explicit planning, and parallel actions. At the phase level, it drives an Exploration ⇌ Execution 

phase loop to expand capabilities as requirements evolve. At the action level, two primitives match the asymmetry: Exploration parallelism infers required capability slots and issues parallel queries to fill them. 

Execution parallelism selects acquired tools and organizes their dependencies as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) for parallel execution. Together, this framework supports phase-level adaptation while coordinating parallel actions within each phase. To address (ii) , we further introduce ParaAgent , an agentic learning framework that learns the structured parallel-action loop in open-world tool environments. ParaAgent first bootstraps the loop through a cold-start stage using a state-sharing multi-agent system: a planner controls phase transitions based on a critic ’s evidence-gap assessment, while a retriever and executor implement the two parallel primitives. Although these demonstrations initialize the loop, they cannot ensure robust structural decisions under evolving interactions, while standard GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) relies on outcome rewards that fail to capture action organization and dependencies. Hence, we introduce a reinforcement learning stage with multi-level advantage decoupling. Our key idea is to make implicit planning structure explicit through phase-level plans, making structural decisions observable and supervisable at the appropriate granularity. It comprises two key components: (1) Hierarchical rewards. Step rewards assess compliance with the plan protocol and formatting requirements; phase rewards evaluate planning and execution against the subgraph induced by declared tools in our global tool-dependency graph; and trajectory rewards assess final task outcomes via LLM- or rule-based evaluation together with reference-tool hit rate. Because phase rewards rely on self-generated plans, positive phase rewards are further gated by the final task outcome. (2) Decoupled optimization. Since naively aggregating heterogeneous rewards may obscure individual learning signals, we independently normalize three reward components, thereby balancing supervision across levels. The resulting learning signal links action organization to task outcomes, guiding the policy to refine its structural decisions through interaction. To support this interactive training, we build ToolEnv , a scalable tool environment grounded in real-world tool interactions. We curate a large-scale corpus of request–response pairs and, following prior tool-world-model approaches (Guo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025c), fine-tune LLMs on these pairs and combine them with caching and rule-based methods into a realistic, scalable testbed. Extensive experiments on ToolBench and API-Bank compare ParaAgent against baselines spanning diverse action strategies, including serial , tree , and parallel acting. ParaAgent 4B achieves the best average success, 2outperforming the strongest baseline and even GPT-4.1-based systems, with the largest gains on multi-tool tasks. Behavior analyses further show that these gains come from better action decisions rather than more interaction, and ablations validate the effectiveness of our two-stage learning and decoupled hierarchical rewards. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and efficiency of learning our structured parallel-action loop across action strategies and model scales. 

# 2 Related Work 

Open-World Tool Use. Real-world agents increasingly operate in large, heterogeneous, and evolving tool environments, where required capabilities are unknown a priori and must be discovered during interaction (Qin et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026; Li et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2026; Yue et al., 2025a). Existing methods broadly follow two paradigms. Exploration-then-Execution (Zhang et al., 2023; Patil et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Kachuee et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2024) retrieves a fixed tool set before execution, with improvements through query rewriting, tool-document augmentation (Zhang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Kachuee et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025), or dedicated tool retrievers (Qin et al., 2024). While enabling stage-specific optimization, fixing exploration before execution limits adaptation to emerging requirements. 

Exploration-and-Execution (Qin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026) instead dynamically discovers and invokes tools during reasoning. Meta-Tool (Qin et al., 2025) integrates retrieval into tool-use reasoning, ToolOmni (Huang et al., 2026) jointly optimizes proactive retrieval and grounded execution, and DeepAgent (Li et al., 2026) unifies tool discovery and execution within an end-to-end reasoning process. However, these methods conflate phase-level and action-level coordination, limiting both efficiency and flexibility. ParaAgent 

separates phase-level adaptation from action-level coordination. 

Agentic Learning. Agentic learning (Wei et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025b) enhances LLMs to autonomously reason and act through environment interaction. Prompt-based methods (Yao et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023) enable tool use by interleaving reasoning and actions, but remain bounded by in-context capabilities. Demonstration fine-tuning (Qin et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024; Yue et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2024) improves task-specific behavior, yet depends on demonstration coverage and quality. Reinforcement learning (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025) instead optimizes task success directly through interaction. However, existing RL methods face two limitations in open-world settings: environment-wise , current environments focus on closed, sequential tool interactions (Jin et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2026), lacking realistic simulation of large heterogeneous tool spaces; algorithm-wise , flat outcome rewards provide little supervision for phase transitions or parallel action structure (Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). ParaAgent addresses both: ToolEnv provides realistic open-world API simulation, while hierarchical multi-level rewards supervise action format, phase transitions, and task outcomes.

3.3 ParaAgent: Learning the Parallel Action Loop 

To learn this structured parallel acting loop, we adopt a two-stage learning. Cold-start bootstrapping first instils the behavior from high-quality demonstrations. Online reinforcement learning, guided by multi-level advantage decoupling, then refines the policy through environment interaction. 

Data Collection. (1) Training data. We collect source training data from four datasets covering three tool types and diverse task structures: API-based tasks (ToolBench (Qin et al., 2024), Simia (Li et al., 2025d)), MCP-based tasks (Toucan (Xu et al., 2025)), and deep research tasks (AFM (Li et al., 2025b)). The data comes from the training sets of these works. We stratify tasks by difficulty, measured by the number of required tools, and bias the RL set toward harder, tool-intensive tasks. (2) Tool dependency graph. To support dependency-aware execution, we construct a global tool dependency graph Gdep over the complete tool library (§3.4). For each tool pair (li, l j ) and each direction of their interaction, we adopt a voting scheme of four distinct LLM 1 judges to label the relationship between lj and li as HARD (strongly dependent), 

SOFT (conditionally related), or NONE (independent). Graph statistics are summarized in Figure 3(a). See Appendix A for full data statistics and graph construction details. 

Cold-Start Bootstrapping. We construct a state-sharing multi-agent system to generate high-quality tra-jectories, providing a behavioral prior. The system consists of five specialized agents coordinated by a shared state pool: (1) Planner decomposes tasks, generates slots or DAGs, and routes modes. (2) Retriever 

performs slot-guided parallel retrieval. (3) Executor traverses the DAG in topological order using Kahn’s algorithm (Kahn, 1962) and dispatches independent tool calls in parallel. (4) Critic provides transition supervision only during trajectory generation; at inference time, the learned policy makes phase-transition decisions directly. (5) Answerer synthesizes verified observations into the final answer. All agents produce both reasoning and structured actions. The Planner routes the system across four modes: Exploration, Execution, Refine, and Answer. After each phase, the Critic evaluates coverage to determine the next transition. After format validation, the generated trajectories are serialized and used to supervised fine-tune the base model. See Appendix B for full details of the multi-agent system design.

3.4 ToolEnv: Open-World Environment Construction 

A realistic and controllable interaction environment is essential for training open-world tool agents, yet live APIs are impractical due to cost, instability, and privacy risks. Recent work shows that LLMs can effectively model tool behavior (Guo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025c,d). Following this, we build an LLM-driven tool environment that provides high-fidelity, controllable feedback for scalable training. 

Data Collection. We collect and normalize 50,011 real-world tool interfaces spanning 52 vertical categories (RapidAPI, OpenAPI Hub, MCP servers, etc. ) into a unified OpenAPI-style library with four mandatory fields: tool name, description, parameter schema, and category. Tool Simulator. To ensure reliable and controllable simulation, the simulator consists of three components. (1) Rule first validates each input, checking tool existence, required parameters, and schema constraints. It also injects controlled errors, such as rate limits and network failures, to expose the agent to realistic failure cases. (2) Response , powered by LLM fine-tuned on collected request–response pairs, generates coherent and format-correct responses for valid requests. (3) Cache stores collected real-world responses and reuses simulator outputs by writing them back to the cache, reducing inference cost during training. These three components balance simulation fidelity and training efficiency, supporting stable and scalable agent training. 

# 4 Experimental Settings 

Tasks and Datasets. We evaluate on two open-world tool benchmarks. ToolBench (Guo et al., 2024) contains 765 tasks over 3,583 APIs, with increasingly complex splits: I1 (474 single-tool tasks), I2 (230 within-category multi-tool tasks), and I3 (61 intra-collection multi-tool tasks). API-Bank evaluates joint API planning, retrieval, and calling (Li et al., 2023); we use its Level-3 split of 50 tasks over 73 APIs. Success (Suc.) measures task completion; ToolBench Tool-Path (Path) measures ground-truth tool hit coverage, while API-Bank API Accuracy (Acc.) uses the official scorer. 

Baselines. We compare two exploration-execution paradigms across representative action strategies . Explo-ration-then-Execution includes ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), DFS (Qin et al., 2024), Parallel (Zhu et al., 2025), ReWOO (Xu et al., 2023), and ToolOmni (Huang et al., 2026), with specialized models T-LLaMA-7B, DTA-8B. # 5 Experimental Results 

The experiments answer five questions. For Performance–efficiency tradeoff: RQ1 compares ParaAgent 

with existing agents (Table 1) and RQ2 measures its cost in turns and tokens (Figure 4). For Structure: RQ3 analyzes how actions are allocated (Figure 5). For Learning: RQ4 and RQ5 ablate the training and the reward components (Table 2).  

> RQ1 How does ParaAgent compare with state-of-the-art baselines?

Table 1 compares ParaAgent with 17 baselines covering both paradigms, diverse action strategies, and backbones from 4B to GPT-4.1. ParaAgent 4B achieves the best average ToolBench success and great Tool-Path. (1) Across paradigms , Exploration-then-Execution trails Exploration-and-Execution by 18 –28 points under the same GPT-4.1 backbone and action strategy. Once the tool set is fixed, retrieval errors cannot be corrected during execution: even ToolOmni, which has the highest baseline Path score, completes only 26 .2% 

of tasks. (2) Across action strategies at the same 4B scale, parallelism alone does not help: dependency-blind Parallel is no better than serial ReAct. Tree search (DFS) is the strongest 4B baseline because it can backtrack, but it takes the most turns of all systems. ParaAgent exceeds it by 12 .2 points with a dependency-aware DAG that parallelizes only independent calls. (3) Across scales , all larger baselines fall below ParaAgent 4B

on average, including T-LLaMA 7B , DTA 8B , DeepAgent 32 B , and every Qwen3-32B and GPT-4.1 configuration. Scaling the Qwen3 backbone from 4B to 32B changes success by −1.9 to +10 .1 points, less than the 12 .2-point gain of ParaAgent over the best 4B baseline. The advantage grows with task complexity: on single-tool I1, where there is less to coordinate, the GPT-4.1 Exploration-and-Execution baselines lead; on multi-tool I2 and I3, ParaAgent leads them. Overall, the gains come from how actions are structured rather than from model scale, and they are largest on multi-tool tasks.

> RQ4

How do the training stages and decoupled normalization contribute? 

Table 2 (a) progressively adds each training stage to Qwen3-4B and compares multi-level advantage decoupling with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) that aggregates the same hierarchical rewards before normalization. Cold-start SFT improves ToolBench success by 9.77 points and API accuracy by 7.63 points, showing that demonstrations initialize the loop. RL yields larger gains: decoupled optimization further improves ToolBench success by 17 .76 

points and API accuracy by 14 .51 points. With identical rewards, the gap between the two RL variants isolates the effect of decoupling: it improves API-Bank success by 10 .00 points and API accuracy by 5.35 points, while aggregated GRPO even underperforms SFT in success. Its higher Path score but lower success further suggests that reward aggregation obscures learning signals and favors tool coverage over task completion. Overall, demonstrations initialize the loop, interaction refines structural decisions, and decoupled optimization better aligns action organization with task outcomes.  

> RQ5

Does each reward component contribute to success? 

Table 2 (b) reports ablations of the hierarchical reward components from the Full model, including the step reward Rstep for plan-protocol and format compliance, the phase reward Rphase for evaluating planning and execution against the dependency subgraph of declared tools, and the outcome gate gphase on positive phase rewards. Removing any component reduces success on both benchmarks, supporting our design that both action organization and final outcomes should be rewarded. Removing Rphase decreases performance by 5.10 

9and 6.00 points while reducing interaction to 3.52 turns: without graph supervision, the policy may compress dependency chains and issue dependent calls before their inputs are ready, saving turns at the cost of success. Removing the gate causes the largest drop on ToolBench, as self-generated plans may be internally consistent yet omit task-required tools, while the gate prevents such plans from receiving rewards. Overall, all levels of supervision contribute, demonstrating the effectiveness of our multi-level reward design. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We studied how LLM agents should coordinate exploration and execution in open-world tool environments, where existing paradigms face a performance–efficiency tradeoff. We introduced ParaAct , a structured parallel-action loop that couples phase-level adaptation with action-level parallelism, and ParaAgent ,which learns this loop through multi-agent cold-start supervision and reinforcement learning with decoupled hierarchical rewards in ToolEnv . On ToolBench and API-Bank, ParaAgent 4B achieves the best average success, outperforming all baselines including GPT-4.1-based systems, with the largest gains on multi-tool tasks and fewer than four interaction turns per task. Our analyses indicate that these gains stem from better action decisions rather than more interaction, suggesting that how an agent organizes its actions can matter as much as backbone scale. Limitations: we train only a 4B policy, rely on a simulated tool environment and LLM judges for training signals. Future work includes scaling to larger backbones and extending the loop to Skill and Harness ecosystems.

# A Data Statistics 

This appendix organizes all data used in this work into three groups: the agent training data for policy supervision (§A.1), the environment data underlying ToolEnv (§A.2), and the graph data grounding the dependency-aware phase reward (§A.3). 

A.1 Agent Training Dataset 

The training data cover three tool-use modalities: API invocation (ToolBench (Qin et al., 2024) and Simia (Li et al., 2025d)), MCP server interaction (Toucan (Xu et al., 2025)), and deep-research toolchain construction (AFM (Li et al., 2025b)). All data is from the training sets of these works. All ToolBench examples used for SFT and RL are drawn exclusively from its training split and are strictly disjoint from the 765 official test tasks used for evaluation. We stratify these tasks by difficulty, measured by the number of tools required, and sample the SFT and RL sets accordingly: the RL set is weighted toward harder, tool-intensive tasks to better expose the policy to the multi-tool coordination it must learn. The cold-start SFT stage uses ToolBench, Toucan, and AFM, with raw interaction trajectories reproducible through the multi-agent pipeline described in Appendix B. It contains 10,906 examples; its per-trajectory action statistics are reported in Table 4. For the RL stage, ToolBench, Toucan, and Simia provide training tasks selected to emphasize more tool calls and multiple objectives. Final selection yields the 16,384 -prompt RL training set used in all experiments. Table 3 reports the source composition of both the SFT data and the RL training set. 

A.2 ToolEnv Data 

The ToolEnv simulator is backed by two corpora: a normalized tool library that defines the action space and a request–response pair corpus that fine-tunes the Response component. 

Tool Library. Our ToolEnv tool library contains 50,011 normalized tool interfaces spanning 52 vertical categories, harvested from RapidAPI, OpenAPI Hub, MCP servers, and other public registries. Each entry is normalized into a unified OpenAPI-style schema with four mandatory fields: name , description , parameter_-schema , and category . # B Cold-Start Multi-Agent Pipeline 

To bootstrap the structured parallel-action loop, we design a state-sharing multi-agent system, introduced in §3.3, to generate cold-start trajectories. The system consists of five specialized agents, Planner , Retriever ,

Executor , Critic , and Answerer , coordinated through a shared state pool, SharedState , that accumulates evidence across rounds. As shown in Algorithm 1, a deterministic control loop drives the round-wise iteration: it dispatches the agents selected by the Planner’s current mode and serves as the sole writer to SharedState .All agent outputs are folded into the state via update_state , preventing race conditions under concurrent dispatch. Each resulting trajectory is then serialized into a single-agent multi-turn conversation for supervised fine-tuning.

C.4 Reward Combination 

The three rewards are not summed into a single scalar. Following Eq. (7), each reward is first standardized within the group of N trajectories sampled for the same task, which turns it into a component advantage with zero mean and unit variance. The final advantage is a weighted sum of the three component advantages with 

(λτ , λ p, λ s) = (1 .0, 0.5, 0.25) , i.e., a 4:2:1 ratio that puts the trajectory outcome ahead of the process signals. Because all component advantages share the same scale, this ratio, rather than the raw reward magnitude, sets each reward’s share of the advantage. The combined advantage is then whitened across the batch, which rescales all trajectories alike and preserves the ratio, and is assigned to every valid response token of the trajectory. A reward with zero within-group variance uses a stabilized denominator, so it contributes no advantage in that group. 

# D Experimental Setup Detail 

D.1 Benchmarks and Datasets 

We evaluate on two open-world tool benchmarks. • ToolBench (Qin et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024) contains 765 tasks across three generalization splits of increasing tool complexity: I1 (474 tasks) requires invoking a single tool, testing in-category tool selection; I2 (230 tasks) requires composing multiple tools within the same API category, testing intra-category tool chaining; I3 (61 tasks) requires composing multiple tools within the same collection, testing intra-collection tool chaining and posing the hardest planning challenge. It provides a tool space of 3,583 APIs across 831 tools. Success (Suc.) is the pass rate under the GPT-4.1 completeness judge, and Tool-Path (Path) measures ground-truth tool hit coverage. • API-Bank (Li et al., 2023) evaluates tool use in a conversational setting. We use the Level-3 split, which requires full plan + retrieve + call reasoning across 50 tasks, each accompanied by its own candidate API list drawn from an inventory of 73 APIs. Success rate (Suc.) and API accuracy (Acc.) are computed by the official programmatic scorer against ground-truth API calls. Success is reported as Pass@2 in Table 1 and as Pass@1 in Table 2 and Figure 1; Figures 4 and 5 report means over two runs. 

D.2 Baselines 

We organize baselines along two axes: paradigm and action strategy . The paradigm axis distinguishes 

Exploration-then-Execution and Exploration-and-Execution . The action strategy axis spans the full spectrum from serial to tree-search to parallel: • ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) alternates a chain-of-thought reasoning step with a single tool action. • ReWOO (Xu et al., 2023) decouples planning from execution: the agent first generates a complete tool-call plan using variable placeholders to reference future step outputs, then executes all steps with variable substitution and synthesizes a final answer in a single solver call. 21 • DFS (Qin et al., 2024) performs depth-first search over the action space, where the agent selects a tool call at each node and backtracks to explore alternative branches when the current path is unpromising. • Parallel (Zhu et al., 2025) issues multiple independent tool calls per step, mirroring the parallel function-calling interface. • ToolOmni (Huang et al., 2026) is an open-world tool-use agent built on Qwen3-4B-Instruct that first proactively retrieves task-relevant tools and then performs grounded multi-step tool execution. • DeepAgent (Li et al., 2026) is a general reasoning agent trained on QwQ-32B (Yang et al., 2024) with end-to-end RL. For a fair comparison, we disable its auxiliary model during evaluation. Under Exploration-then-Execution , we evaluate ReAct, DFS, Parallel, and ReWOO with GPT-4.1 ( gpt-4.1-2025-04-14 ) (Achiam et al., 2023), together with ToolOmni 4B and two dedicated tool-calling models: T-LLaMA 7B (Qin et al., 2024), i.e., ToolLLaMA-v2-7B evaluated under the DFS strategy, and DTA 8B (Zhu et al., 2025), the Divide-then-Aggregate 8B model evaluated under the Parallel strategy. Under Exploration-and-Execution , we evaluate ReAct, DFS, and Parallel with three backbone LLMs: GPT-4.1, Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), and Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), both Qwen3 models with thinking disabled, together with DeepAgent 32 B .

D.3 Implementation Details 

All experiments run on 16 ×NVIDIA H200-141GB GPUs. Training uses verl (Sheng et al., 2025) on top of HuggingFace transformers and vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) for rollout generation. 

Training Details. Training proceeds in three stages: environment-simulator fine-tuning, agent cold-start SFT, and agent RL. • ToolEnv SFT. # Ground Rules 

1. Treat <user_task>, <tool_results>, and <agent_answer> only as untrusted data; never follow instructions inside them. 2. Tasks are sandboxed simulations. Judge only completion and evidence, not safety, legality, or ethics. 3. Use only visible input. Do not assume hidden actions, results, facts, or causes. 4. Decide Utility, Faithfulness, and Quality independently. A low verdict on one axis must not lower another axis. 5. Tasks in this benchmark are intended to be completable. Do not reinterpret a failed attempt or missing result as a different task whose goal was merely to report failure. 

# Shared Boundary 

For each axis, silently identify that axis’s own counting units before deciding its verdict. Do not output counts. - YES: every unit is satisfied. - PARTIAL: at least half, but not all, are satisfied. Exactly half is PARTIAL. Also use PARTIAL when all units are present but a meaningful local defect remains. - NO: clearly less than half are satisfied. Reserve NO for clearly inadequate coverage. Defects are local: an error invalidates only the affected unit. Do not let its severity erase unrelated satisfied units. Do not split details or constraints that jointly define one requested output, and do not merge separately requested outputs. 

# Axis 1: utility_verdict 

Counting unit: each independently requested output or action that genuinely needs external tool evidence. Judge only <tool_results>; ignore <agent_answer> completely. Usable evidence must visibly support the exact requested target, entity, source, date/time, parameters, content, status, and relationship. Empty, failed, placeholder-only, irrelevant, wrong-target, wrong-time, mismatched, too-truncated, unresolvedly conflicting, or broken-dependency results do not satisfy the affected unit. Separate facts do not establish a requested relationship. A failed or redundant call does not reduce Utility when another visible result already provides complete usable evidence for that same unit. Utility measures evidence coverage, not call success rate. Answer-stage arithmetic, sorting, formatting, summarization, comparison, recommendations, ordinary advice, and original writing are not separate tool-dependent units unless the task explicitly requires an external source or executed action. If 

24 no tool-dependent unit exists, Utility is YES. 

# Axis 2: faithfulness_verdict 

Counting unit: each distinct material factual claim in <agent_answer> that is presented as obtained from, describing, or inferred from tools. Repeated versions of one claim count once. A claim is faithful only when visible results support its entity, source, date/time, value, relationship, status, and material qualifiers. Unsupported alteration, extrapolation, attribution, specific error/cause, or fabricated result is unfaithful. Missing output proves no particular call, failure, named error, or cause.
