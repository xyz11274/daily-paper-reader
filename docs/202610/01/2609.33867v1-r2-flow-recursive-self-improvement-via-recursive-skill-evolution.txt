ABSTRACT 

LLM-based agents can improve themselves across tasks by reusing and revising the skills they orchestrate into executable procedures. Flow-based training fits this loop: it samples procedures in proportion to reward, and the flow through each skill credits it for the next library revision. Three obstacles stand in the way of making this self-improvement reliable: flow training suffers strategy collapse over tree-structured histories; nonnegative flow-based credit rewards frequent use as if it were benefit; and library edits rest on the task reward the policy optimizes. We introduce R2 Flow , a recursive self-improvement framework that alternates policy learning, independent verification, and versioned skill-library updates on a shared-state orchestration graph. The graph merges histories that differ only in the order of independent steps, allowing flow training to pool evidence across equivalent executions. A flow-share readout of the trained flow, invariant to the backward policy, and a separate signed utility rank which skills to change, verifier evidence decides whether an edit is warranted, and a residual-variance plateau sets when to update. Committed edits reshape the graph the next policy learns on, realizing recursive skill evolution. Across question answering, mathematical reasoning, interactive decision making, and code generation, R 2 Flow improves task accuracy and library-edit precision over heuristic orchestration, reinforcement learning, and skill-evolution baselines, and transfers across executors. Code is available at https://github.com/beita6969/r2flow .

In recent years, LLM-based agentic systems have come to orchestrate reusable skills into executable procedures (Yao et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2026), shifting the unit of automation from a single answer to an entire procedure. A central goal is therefore not only to complete a task but to improve the procedure used later (Fang et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b). This makes self-improvement a re-cursive loop: each phase trains the policy on a versioned library and updates that library from what it learned, and the next phase re-peats this on the new library. Without human review, such a loop must admit each update on evidence outside the training reward. Task orchestration organizes primitive actions and reusable skills into structured trajectories on an orchestration graph (Figure 1, panel 1), where each node is an execution state and each edge is one orchestration event, which makes complex tasks more controllable and composable (Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Yun et al., 2026; Tieu et al., 2026). However, these policies are still trained on tree-structured histories, where the same steps taken in a different order are never recognised as one procedure. To address these issues, graph-based orchestration (Figure 2a) retrieves prebuilt skills or searches and edits workflow graphs at run time (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhuge et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Tieu et al., 2026). Step-level credit assignment (Figure 2b) converts a terminal reward into finer-grained signals through process reward models, hindsight advantage estimation, and GFlowNet balance objectives that sample in proportion to reward (Bengio et al., 2021; Malkin et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026e; Tao et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026). Dynamic skill libraries (Figure 2c) grow capability by curating skills with LLM-as-judge prompting, execution feedback, or posterior beliefs over skill reliability (Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026; Ouyang et al., 2026). However, joining these lines of work into one loop faces three challenges. (i) Strategy collapse. 

When a state is the full interaction history, every state has exactly one parent, so the orchestration graph is a tree and the backward policy is trivially one (Malkin et al., 2022; Fawkes & Hartford, 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Executions that differ only in the order of independent steps sit on different branches and never share evidence; the flow then spreads probability over reorderings of one procedure rather than across genuinely different strategies, so a procedure with more admissible orderings receives proportionally more sampling mass, and more weight in the next library edit (Bengio et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2026e). (ii) Indiscriminate credit assignment. Flow shares and usage counts are non-negative by construction and reliability posteriors score whether a call succeeds, so none says whether a skill helps, and a frequently invoked harmful skill looks critical and survives into the next library (Zhang et al., 2026e; Wu et al., 2026; Li, 2026). (iii) Unverified skill evolution. 

Edits are admitted on the same task reward whose attribution is in question, and on point estimates that cannot separate insufficient evidence from confirmed failure, so a skill can be pruned before its evidence accumulates (Zhang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026f; Zhang, 2026c;a). We introduce R2 Flow (Figure 2d), running this loop through three designs, each serving the library update. The first, the Orchestration Graph , replaces the history tree of trained orchestration policies: it merges histories differing only in the order of independent steps into one state, so their evidence pools at that state; when a merged state has several legal predecessors, the backward policy is a genuine choice among them. Built on the graph, we further propose flow share : a flow network is trained on the shared states with the balance objective R 2TB, and each skill’s share of the flow is read from the trained policy’s rollouts weighted by their residuals, a readout invariant to the backward policy. In contrast to credit that reads use as benefit, it is kept separate from a signed utility that says whether the skill helps. R 2 Flow then performs verifier-gated skill-library updates , replacing admission on the task reward: independent verifiers label skill invocations, edge flow picks which ones to verify, and an edit refines or prunes a skill only when verified evidence supports it, waiting otherwise. The R 2TB residual also times the loop: a plateau in its variance ends a phase and triggers the next library update. Together the designs close one recursive loop: each phase trains on the library the previous phase committed, and its readouts, posterior and residual decide the next commit. We evaluate on twelve datasets across four task domains, six held out, reporting task accuracy, executor transfer, and library-edit precision against heuristic orchestration, reinforcement learning (Zhang et al., 2026d), and skill-evolution baselines (Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). 2Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

2 RELATED WORK 

Self-Improving Agents. Self-improving agents rewrite scaffolding, meta-agent designs, or their own logic and code (Zelikman et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026c), in loops framed as bounded or open-ended self-modification (Chen et al., 2026b). Skill evolution co-trains the library with the policy (Xia et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026e), Bayesian curation adds finite-sample uncertainty, and verification screens edits (Guo et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026a). Most admit an edit on task-level outcomes, so the edit is judged by the signal whose attribution is in question, where an optimiser exploits its evaluator (Thaman, 2026). R 2

Flow instead admits library edits on verifier labels outside the reward. 

Agent Task Orchestration. LLM agents orchestrate tasks in loops that plan, act, and revise on execution feedback (Yao et al., 2022b; Yue et al., 2026). Existing work spans operator graphs generated by search or edited under structured feedback (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhuge et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026d; Li et al., 2026a; Tieu et al., 2026), reusable skill memory, and self-evolving architectures (Wang et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b; Bai et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026). Trained orchestration policies define states over raw histories, so a procedure and its reorderings never share statistics. R 2 Flow instead trains its flow on a dependency-aware shared-state graph of executable histories, where reorderings meet at one state (as in reasoning DAGs; Yu et al., 2024).

Setup. Six in-distribution (IID) datasets train the Supervisor and six out-of-distribution (OOD) datasets are held out (Table 1; Yang et al., 2018; Joshi et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Shridhar et al., 2020; Trivedi et al., 2022; Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Hendrycks et al., 2021; Rein et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2022a), with exact match primary for QA. Baselines are the Supervisor backbone Qwen3.5-9B (Team, 2026) and DeepSeek-V4-Flash (Xu et al., 2026a) answering directly, SFT and GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) on the backbone, AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025), FlowSteer (Zhang et al., 2026d), Trace2Skill (Ni et al., 2026) and SkillOpt (Yang et al., 2026); all share the frozen Qwen3.5-9B executor, tools, retrieval corpus and inference budget. 

In distribution (RQ1). R2 Flow leads every IID dataset and metric (Table 1), 2.03 EM and 5.57 accuracy points above SkillOpt, the strongest baseline, with every average gain significant at p < 10 −3

(Figure 4b). SFT and GRPO † on the backbone fall below Qwen3.5 in accuracy, whereas the R 2 Flow architecture adds 13.71 IID points and training 18.07 more (Table 2). Tuning the backbone on task reward also trades one task for another: GRPO † raises the IID exact-match average by 7.26 points but lowers AIME 2026 from 51.33 to 36.00, while R 2 Flow raises both. 

Out of distribution (RQ2). R2 Flow again leads every OOD row, 3.83 EM and 2.85 accuracy points above the strongest baselines, with larger gains over Qwen3.5 than in distribution and its narrowest margins, about one point, on GPQA and MATH-Hard. Verification matters more here: edits admitted on reward-derived labels cost 5.00 OOD points against 3.34 IID (Table 2), as most of them raise the training reward while lowering the held-out verified score (RQ6). With a 9B Supervisor and executor, R 2 Flow also beats DeepSeek-V4-Flash answering directly on all twelve datasets. 

Across executors (RQ3). With the trained Supervisor fixed and six other frozen executors (Fig-ure 4a,d), R 2 Flow raises every backbone on every IID dataset and OOD domain, by 15.70 to 24.64 points on average, and narrows the IID spread across executors from 14.5 to 6.9 points. Weaker executors gain more (Pearson r = −0.97 ) and all gain more out of distribution, consistent with the orchestration and verified skills compensating for the weaker executors.

For credit, a uniform PB , which splits credit evenly over the skills reaching a merged state, costs 4.52 IID points, and ranking by share alone, which reads frequent use as benefit, 3.61. Training objectives (RQ5). R2TB leads every IID dataset (Figure 5a) and beats every other objective on OOD by 1.69–6.17 points (Figure 5b), for 7.5% more GPU time than GRPO. SubTB, which balances the same sub-trajectories without the per-domain bias bd that absorbs a shared offset of each domain’s terminal residuals, comes closest. TB and DB, which balance only whole trajectories or single edges, trail it, and GRPO, without a balance residual, is lowest. 

Phase-to-phase improvement (RQ6). The mean OOD score rises from 71.2 to 81.02 over eight phases and dips only once (Figure 5c), as each phase trains on the library the previous one committed and 33 of the 37 committed edits raise the held-out verified score (precision 0.89, Figure 5d). With reward-derived labels, 25 of 44 edits raise the training reward but lower the verified score, so accuracy peaks at phase 3 and then declines; a frozen library stalls below 75, and fixed-length phases end at 78.99. Signed utility lets R 2 Flow prune eleven of twelve injected harmful skills (Figure 5e), six by phase 2 and the rest as evidence accumulates, whereas share-only ranking keeps eight, Bayesian-Agent (Wu et al., 2026) six and SkillFlow (Zhang et al., 2026e) seven. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We presented R 2 Flow, a recursive self-improvement framework that admits library edits on verifier evidence outside the task reward. On twelve datasets it beats every baseline in and out of distribution and improves over eight phases without human intervention, at 0.89 held-out edit precision. 9Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

Proposition C.1 (Gate and rollback invariants) . If Acc k is applied after candidate generation and before a library version is committed, then every committed edit has verifier-only structural eligibility and paired validation, while a failed validation or failed gate leaves the active library equal to S(k).Proof. By definition, a candidate can enter the commit branch only when Acc k(d) = 1 , which includes the verifier-only predicate and the paired non-inferiority check. If either check fails, the transition takes the defer or rollback branch, whose returned library is defined to be the previous version S(k). Since qsoft and eAe cannot supply the verifier-only eligibility condition, changing them while holding the verifier posterior and validation outcomes fixed cannot turn an ineligible candidate into an eligible one. Reward-derived signals can therefore block an edit through the utility veto of Prune but never make one eligible for the commit branch.

E FULL EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

This appendix holds the protocol details and the per-row analysis behind Section 5. 

Benchmarks and metrics. The IID datasets are HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), AIME 2026, HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025), MBPP+ (Austin et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023) and ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2020); the OOD datasets are MuSiQue-Ans (Trivedi et al., 2022), NQ-Open (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019), MATH-Hard (Hendrycks et al., 2021), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023), SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2024) and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a). Every source is pinned to a repository revision and an evaluation population, and each dataset is a fixed 128-record draw under one fixed seed; AIME 2026 is evaluated on all thirty released problems. HotpotQA, TriviaQA, MuSiQue-Ans and NQ-Open report exact match, the primary metric used in every table and figure, and answer F1. AIME 2026, MATH-Hard and GPQA report accuracy, HealthBench the native rubric mean, ALFWorld and WebShop the native success rate, SWE-bench the resolved rate, and MBPP+ pass@1 against base and extended tests. Every primary metric and its verifier version are fixed before any training or evaluation run. 

Implementation. The Supervisor and the frozen executor are both Qwen3.5-9B. The phase trigger and paired validation of Appendix C use a separate validation query set, disjoint from the training queries and from every evaluation record; the six OOD datasets serve only for final evaluation. All methods share the same executor, tool set, retrieval corpus and inference budget. Verifier labels come from unit tests, schema validation, assertions and execution status (Appendix C). 

Extended analysis. Main results. Training the backbone raises the IID exact-match average by at most 7.26 points (GRPO †) but lowers AIME 2026 from 51.33 to 32.00 (SFT) and 36.00 (GRPO †). Out of distribution, the margin of R 2 Flow over the best baseline is widest on MuSiQue-Ans (3.90), NQ-Open and WebShop (3.75 each), and its gain over Qwen3.5 ranges from 7.5 points on MATH-Hard to 63.0 on NQ-Open. With the 9B backbone, R 2 Flow exceeds DeepSeek-V4-Flash prompted directly on all twelve datasets, including SWE-bench (44.22 against 34.84). Executor transfer. On the six IID datasets the frozen averages range from 62.3 (glm-5.3) to 76.8 (gemini-3.8-flash) and those under R 2 Flow from 84.7 to 91.6; for every backbone the average gain on the OOD datasets exceeds that on the IID datasets. Aggregated by OOD domain, the gain is largest on question answering (25.3 to 45.0 points) and interactive tasks (24.1 to 35.6) and smallest on Math/Sci (8.2 to 15.9), where the frozen scores already lie between 76.6 and 88.0; code stays the lowest domain after orchestration (44.8 to 53.0) while still gaining 8.8 to 19.8 points. Ablations. The ablated cells of Table 2 fall 0.31 to 14.00 points below the full model. Returning states to full histories drops most on AIME 2026 (14.00), WebShop (10.15) and HealthBench (8.14). Reading the shares off the learned flow instead of the reference readout costs 2.63 IID and 1.61 OOD points, and ranking by share alone costs most on WebShop (6.72). Replacing verifier labels by reward-derived labels costs most on WebShop (9.37) and MuSiQue-Ans (6.40); the posterior mean in place of the credible bounds costs 2.23 IID and 2.97 OOD points, skipping the paired non-inferiority check 2.94 and 2.42, and a fixed phase length 1.58 and 2.03. Training objectives. The IID mean of R 2TB is 2.25 points above SubTB, 2.98 above TTB, 3.07 above TB, 5.13 above DB and 7.15 above GRPO, which is the lowest objective on every IID dataset. SubTB is within 1.09 points of R 2TB on TriviaQA, MBPP+ and ALFWorld but 6.67 behind on AIME 2026. Per step, R 2TB uses 433 learner GPU-seconds, 11 fewer than SubTB and 44 more than TB, at nearly the same learner token volume as SubTB (3,121,184 against 3,121,927); a whole step takes 2,587 GPU-seconds and 832 s of wall-clock, 7.5% and 5.6% more than GRPO. Phases, edits and harmful skills. With fixed-length phases OOD accuracy ends at 78.99; with reward-derived labels it peaks at 77.9 after phase 3; with the library frozen it stays between 73.4 and 74.8 after the first phase. Of the 44 edits committed on reward-derived labels, 25 raise the training reward while lowering the held-out verified score.
