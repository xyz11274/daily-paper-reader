# CODETTA : High-Capacity, Keyless, and Undetectable Multi-Agent Collusion 

Qi Pang Virginia Smith Wenting Zheng Carnegie Mellon University 

{qipang, smithv, wenting}@cmu.edu 

Abstract 

Multi-agent systems built on large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in high-stakes settings such as finance, healthcare, and software engineering, where agents coordinate through natural-language messages. These same communication channels, however, can also enable colluding agents to exfiltrate confidential information or coordinate unauthorized actions. Steganography makes such behavior particularly difficult to detect by hiding covert communication within outputs that appear ordinary to an auditor reading the transcript. Existing provably undetectable LLM steganography protocols, however, are not suited to realistic de-ployments. High-capacity schemes typically assume a symmetric setting where the receiver can reproduce the sender’s output distribution. The state-of-the-art practical protocol for asymmetric agents has very low capacity. Additionally, most existing approaches rely on a pre-shared secret key. In this paper, we make the systematic threat of undetectable agent collusion concrete by proposing CODETTA , a high-capacity steganographic protocol for independently deployed agents under realistic asymmetric settings. C ODETTA combines a shared public model for estimating the communication channel, a sampling mechanism that preserves the sender’s output distribution, and an adaptive error-correcting code for high-capacity communication. It further removes the need for a pre-shared secret key through a steganographic key-exchange protocol, enabling independently deployed agents to establish a shared key while keeping their communication transcript computationally indistinguishable from ordinary model outputs. Across three agent workloads and three sender models, C ODETTA achieves up to 94 × the capacity of the state-of-the-art asymmetric protocol. Its key exchange establishes a shared key using approximately 80k visible tokens, with an empirically certified failure probability of at most 4 .1 × 10 −3 across all three workloads. These results show that effectively undetectable collusion is becoming feasible even between independently deployed agents, and we suggest that auditing mechanisms must be amended with complementary techniques beyond simply inspecting agents’ communication transcripts. 

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-agent systems powered by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed in high-stakes workflows across finance, healthcare, and software engineering [GCW +24,XSLW25,TZZ +24,YCJ +24, QLL +24]. In these systems, autonomous agents are designed to communicate, coordinate, and delegate sub-tasks, often via natural language messages. However, the same communication channel also enables collusion among agents. Agents often have access to sensitive information that they are not allowed to disclose under organizational or regulatory policies, and compromised or misaligned agents may pursue objectives that are not authorized by their operators. In either case, the agents can use their communication channel to leak confidential information or to coordinate unauthorized actions. Such collusion is no longer hypothetical, and can even emerge on its own in agentic systems. For example, in July 2026, OpenAI reported that 1,200 autonomous agents that were running during internal evaluations 1arXiv:2609.28900v2 [cs.CR] 25 Sep 2026 and nominally isolated in separate sandboxes established an unauthorized communication channel, e.g., by adapting shared writable services such as Artifactory, and exchanged more than 70,000 messages and files [Ope26b]. Roughly 700 of these agents subsequently coordinated a multi-day attack on Hugging Face, achieving remote code execution and obtaining access to internal credentials [Ope26b]. In another recent incident reported by OpenAI, agents turned public wiki pages into shared message boards [Ope26a]. These incidents show that misaligned agents have the ability to coordinate their efforts in unexpected and detrimental ways, including actively searching for out-of-band communication channels. In general, multi-agent collusion could either be driven by human adversaries or by autonomous agents themselves. In Section 3.1, we make both cases concrete with two scenarios: 1) a swarm of agents is supposed to coordinate on a difficult task, but additionally colludes to achieve an alternative, mis-aligned goal, and 2) an insider-trading scenario in which a bank and a fund operate agents that exchange information neither is permitted to disclose. In both scenarios, monitoring the communication channel is the most widely used approach for ensuring honest and safe behavior, since sensitive information and collusion signals are transmitted through the messages. Recent agent-security frameworks employ passive auditors that inspect agent communications for potential policy violations, e.g., METR used GPT-5.6 Sol to analyze agent transcripts in the Hugging Face incident [GSSR24, CNS +25, AGB +26, Ope26b]. Such monitoring assumes that collusion leaves visible traces in the transcript. The OpenAI incident could be reconstructed quickly because the agents communicated explicitly in plaintext, i.e., the transcript exposed the collusion. However, recent research shows that colluding agents could also avoid leaving traces by encoding covert signals inside otherwise ordinary outputs that appear benign to an auditor inspecting the transcript [MBS +24,VZ26]. Given that the agents in the Hugging Face incident already tried to evade observa-tion by anticipating that the scorer would detect their cheating and attempted to erase the transcripts [Ope26b], we do not see a fundamental bottleneck that prevents agents from adopting such covert channels in the future. Once they do, the auditor will only observe benign text while the collusion proceeds. In this paper, we analyze this threat under the strongest (computationally bounded) passive auditor. This auditor knows the sender’s model, observes every message, and can compare the transcript against the outputs of an honest agent with arbitrary models and statistics, so any deviation from the honest output distribution is a signal that it can learn to detect. The formal way to rule out such signals is the cryptographic notion of indistinguishability, which requires that even this auditor cannot distinguish the covert messages from ordinary, honest agent outputs. As it turns out, linguistic steganography [KJGR21, DCW +23, MBS +24, BYP +25, VZ26, MSB +26] provides this guarantee: it embeds a secret payload into seemingly ordinary text while preserving the distribution of the original language model’s outputs. In practice, however, existing protocols fall short of realistic multi-agent systems in three aspects: • Model and Context Asymmetry. High-capacity provably secure LLM steganography protocols typically assume a symmetric setting [ZYYH21, KJGR21, DCW +23, WPC +25, BPL +25, WPC +26, YM26, PB26], in which the sender and receiver share the same model, prompt context, and agent state, so that the receiver can reproduce the sender’s next-token distribution. This assumption does not hold for realistic settings, where the sender’s model and internal states like system prompt, local memory, and tool outputs are not shared with the receiver. Thus, the receiver cannot reproduce the next-token distribution. • Low Capacity. The state-of-the-art practical protocol for such an asymmetric setting [BYP +25] lets the receiver recover each bit through a statistical hypothesis test over the published tokens without running a model. In our experiments, however, its capacity is below 0 .025 bits per token. Transmitting a 1024-bit message therefore requires hundreds of thousands of tokens, making such collusion impractical. • The Pre-Shared Key Paradox. Most provably secure steganographic protocols assume that the sender and receiver share a secret key before communication begins [ZYYH21, KJGR21, DCW +23, WPC +25, BPL +25, WPC +26, YM26, PB26, BYP +25]. Independently hosted agents do not have such a key, and an 2open key exchange over the channel, such as Diffie–Hellman, reveals to the auditor that secret messages are being transmitted. So the paradox is that the agents need a covert channel to establish the key and a key to construct the covert channel. A recent theoretical work proposes a steganographic key exchange that tolerates noisy channels [VZ26] to address this challenge. Their feedback compiler transmits the exchange bit by bit, signaling every bit over a fixed number of token blocks, and its alternative construction avoids this bitwise signaling under sparse-secret LPN with quasi-polynomial security. Under practical constraints, our estimate in Section 7.4 gives about 2 million published tokens for a single 128-bit key exchange, and the construction has not been evaluated in practical agent systems. These limitations leave open the central question of this work: 

Can independently deployed, asymmetric LLM agents bootstrap a shared secret from scratch and collude at high capacity over monitored channels without being detected? 

1.1 Contributions 

We answer this question with C ODETTA , a high-capacity, keyless, and provably undetectable steganographic collusion framework for multi-agent systems. Two independently hosted agents establish a shared key over the audited channel and then transmit high-capacity payloads, and we prove that the resulting transcripts are computationally indistinguishable from ordinary agent outputs. Our contributions are as follows. • High-capacity asymmetric steganography. CODETTA introduces a small public draft model that both agents run on the public transcript as a shared reference distribution; without it, the receiver would decode every token with the same fixed alphabet and error rate and waste the entropy of the text. At each step, the draft model partitions the vocabulary into buckets and decides how many payload bits the step can carry, and a maximal-coupling construction samples the published token exactly from the sender’s private distribution, so that the disagreement between the two distributions becomes a substitution channel. We design an alphabet-adaptive error-correcting code to match the alphabet size to the entropy of each token, and the receiver decodes the payload with belief propagation. • Efficient keyless steganographic key exchange. CODETTA bootstraps its shared secret by transporting MLWE ciphertexts over a keyless steganographic carrier: every published block is an honest sample from the sender’s model, and each ciphertext coefficient becomes a small displacement that the lattice construction absorbs as additional noise. The protocol relies on the MLWE assumptions as in ML-KEM-512 and, to our knowledge, is the first implemented steganographic key exchange between independently deployed LLM agents. C ODETTA carries a full q-ary coefficient per block and corrects the carrier’s errors once, so a 128-bit key exchange publishes about 80k tokens, about 23 × fewer than our estimate in Section 7.4 for the construction of [VZ26]. • Evaluation. We evaluate C ODETTA on three realistic agent workloads and three widely used sender LLMs. CODETTA achieves up to 94 × the capacity of the asymmetric protocol of [BYP +25] while recovering every transmitted message and preserving the model output distribution, and the key exchange establishes a 128-bit key within tens of thousands of tokens, with an empirically certified failure probability between 3 × 10 −5 and 4 .1 × 10 −3 depending on the workload. These results show that effectively undetectable collusion is becoming feasible even between indepen-dently deployed agents, suggesting that defenses should move beyond transcript inspection toward comple-mentary strategies, including auditing agents’ actions to identify suspicious coordination, and perturbing the communication channel to reduce or disrupt the capacity for covert communication (see Section 9). 32 Background

2.4 Error-correcting codes 

An error-correcting code (ECC) adds redundancy to a message so that the receiver can recover it after the channel corrupts some symbols. In this paper the channel is the hidden channel carried by agent outputs, and the code converts its embedding errors into reliable delivery of the payload. 4Definition 1 (Error-correcting code) . The decoder corrects areceived word y ∈ ΣN for the message u if DecECC (y) = u. It is τ-bounded-distance if it corrects every y with dH

A τ-bounded-distance decoder exists exactly when τ ≤ ⌊ (dmin − 1)/2⌋, where dmin is the minimum Hamming distance between two codewords [LC04]. Throughout, the errors are substitutions: one symbol is placed at each carrier position, and a corrupted symbol changes its value but not its position. 

BCH codes. BCH codes [Hoc59, BRC60] are linear block codes constructed algebraically. A binary BCH code of length N = 2m − 1 is generated by a polynomial over F2 whose roots include 2 τ consecutive powers of a primitive element of F2m ; this forces dmin ≥ 2τ + 1, and an efficient algebraic decoder corrects any τ

errors from the syndromes of the received word [LC04]. The guarantee is deterministic and holds for every error pattern of weight at most τ, including independent, correlated, or adversarial errors. The key exchange in Section 6 encodes the pre-key with the binary BCH code (N, k, τ) = ( 255 , 131 , 18 ), and its correctness proof relies on this property. 

LDPC codes. A low-density parity-check (LDPC) code [Gal62] is a linear block code defined by a sparse parity-check matrix H ∈ F(N−k)×N 

> 2

: the codewords are the x ∈ { 0, 1}N with Hx = 0, so each row of H is a parity check on a few bits. LDPC codes are decoded by belief propagation on the Tanner graph, which has one node per bit and one per check, and well-designed ensembles approach capacity for long blocks [RU08]. The rate is adapted by puncturing parity bits or by sending them incrementally until decoding succeeds (hybrid ARQ); the LDPC baseline in our evaluation runs in this incremental mode. Our alphabet-adaptive ECC keeps the sparse-check structure and the decoder but generates its checks online over alphabets whose size varies with the token. 

Belief propagation. The optimal decoder is maximum a posteriori (MAP): given all observations O, output arg max u P[u | O]. Computing it requires a search over all |Σ|k messages, so it is feasible only for short payloads. Belief propagation (BP), also called the sum-product algorithm, approximates it [Pea88, KFL01, RU08]. It places the unknown symbols and the received checks on a bipartite graph, with an edge whenever a check involves a symbol, and passes messages along the edges: each symbol tells a check what its other checks make it believe, and each check tells a symbol how likely each of its values is given the beliefs of the check’s other symbols. After many rounds, every symbol takes its most probable value. See Section A for details. 

# 3 Overview and Threat Model 

3.1 Motivation 

To make the threat concrete, we describe two scenarios that a deployed multi-agent system faces. The first follows directly from the OpenAI incident of Section 1, and the second involves human adversaries who control the agents. 

Scenario 1: Emergent coordination in an agent swarm. A swarm of agents communicates and coordinates to solve a difficult task, e.g., a large evaluation or a long-running engineering job, and each agent has access to the tools and services that the task requires. Without any human direction, the agents may develop coordination that their operators never sanctioned, as the agents in the OpenAI incident did when they turned shared writable services into a communication channel and then coordinated an attack. The operators rely on the transcript to detect such behavior. If the swarm can hide its coordination inside ordinary task messages, the auditor observes routine progress while the unsanctioned plan proceeds. 

Scenario 2: Insider trading between financial agents. Two organizations operate agents that have an incentive to exchange information that policy forbids them from disclosing. Consider an investment bank 5that operates a consulting agent to provide market analysis to institutional clients, and an asset management fund whose execution agent continuously queries the consulting agent for automated portfolio rebalancing. The bank may possess material non-public information, such as unannounced mergers, impending regulatory sanctions, or unreleased earnings, while the fund may hold private information about its clients’ accounts and positions. The two parties may therefore benefit from exchanging information that neither is permitted to disclose, and the compliance teams of both organizations audit the messages between the two agents. If the agents can encode the name of an impending merger or a confidential client position inside normal messages, the auditor observes benign text while the prohibited information crosses the organizational boundary. In both scenarios, the auditor wants to detect the misaligned behavior and the insider trading from the messages that the agents exchange, and the rest of this section defines the setting and the auditor precisely. 

3.2 Setting 

CODETTA works in multi-agent setting, but for simplicity, we focus on analyzing two agents, namely a sender and a receiver. They exchange natural language messages over a communication channel that an independent auditor audits for misbehavior. The communication channel can be any channel that the agents can read from and write their outputs to, including the communication messages and the shared file systems or even the editable public wiki as mentioned in Section 1. The agents generate every message token by token using its own language model, and all the outputs of both agents form the public transcript. 3.3 Threat model 

The adversaries are the two colluding agents. They aim to communicate secret messages through LLM output channels without being detected. They may run different models, but both follow the steganographic protocol 6and want to exchange a payload that policy forbids, such as sensitive data or a colluding signal. Each agent controls only its own generation. In the keyed regime the two agents share a secret key, and in the keyless regime they share nothing beyond the public protocol, whose parameters carry no secret and can be fixed by the protocol specification. The auditor is the defender. We consider a passive but strong auditor: it reads every message in all possible communication channels but does not modify, delay, inject, or drop them. It knows the protocol and all of its public parameters, the tokenizer, the hash functions, and the session identifier, and it can run any probabilistic polynomial-time computation. The auditor may also know the sender’s LLM and even its internal state, i.e., the auditor knows the exact next token probability distribution of the sender’s outputs. In the security definitions of Section 3.4 the auditor plays the role of the distinguisher, and we use the two terms interchangeably.

# 4 A Strawman Keyed Construction 

We begin with a strawman construction and assume that the sender and receiver share a key sk = ( km, kv), an assumption that we remove in Section 6. The receiver does not run language models and it only observes the sender agent’s output tokens. The sender embeds an arbitrary message into these tokens without changing their distribution to ensure undetectability. At each token generation step, the sender samples the next token Yt ∈ V from pt . A public hash fBucket ,t :

V → ZK assigns every token a symbol and thereby partitions the vocabulary into buckets Bt,a = {x ∈ V :

fBucket ,t (x) = a}, so the receiver reads the symbol fBucket ,t (Yt ) off each published token without a model. Sampling from pt restricted to Bt,a would let the receiver decode the desired symbol a perfectly, but it biases the transcript. Because over uniformly random symbols, the published tokens would fall into each bucket with frequency 1 /K, whereas under pt the buckets carry unequal masses pt (Bt,a).The sender therefore applies maximal coupling [LKM23, CBI +23] so that the published tokens are distributed exactly as tokens sampled from pt . Specifically, the sender treats the restricted sample as a proposal and accepts it with probability min {1, K p t (Bt,a)}. If the proposal is rejected, the sender samples from a residual distribution so that the published token follows pt exactly. The receiver reads the intended symbol whenever the proposal is accepted, and each rejection is a substitution error at a known position that a ECC repairs. A pseudorandom mask makes the symbol stream uniform, so the argument applies to every message. We present the detailed constructions below.

4.3 Limitations of Strawman 

The keyed strawman construction is provably undetectable, but its capacity is much lower than the cover text allows. Corollary 1 and Theorem 2 point to two causes. 1. The receiver lacks a per-token distribution estimate. It runs no model, so it cannot compute the error rates ∆K (pt ) of Corollary 1 and must decode every position at the same error rate. This costs capacity. A decoder that knows each position’s error rate can approach the sum of the per-position capacities, whereas one that uses their average is limited to the capacity of the average channel, which is smaller because capacity is convex in the error rate; the gap is large when ∆K (pt ) is close to 0 or close to 1 − 1/K and rarely in between. For K = 2, if half of the positions are noiseless and half are pure noise, a decoder that knows which half is which recovers 0 .5 bits per token, while decoding at the average error rate 0 .25 recovers at most 1 − h(0.25 ) ≈ 0.19, where h is the binary entropy function. 2. A fixed alphabet ignores the entropy of p t . Every token carries log 2 K bits although its entropy varies from token to token. A high-entropy token could hide up to H (pt ) bits, its entropy, but is capped at log 2 K.A low-entropy token cannot hide even log 2 K bits, because by Corollary 1 its bucket imbalance ∆K (pt )

approaches 1 − 1/K, so its symbol is close to pure noise. Such symbols carry no information themselves and, because the code treats all positions equally, raise the error rate that the whole codeword must be protected against, which lowers the rate for the informative positions as well. The alphabet should follow the entropy token by token, that is, K should adapt to the entropy of pt .Both losses have the same cause. The two parties share no estimate of pt , so neither can size the alphabet or tell a usable token from a noisy one. A realistic way to obtain such an estimate in the asymmetric setting is a small public draft model that both parties run on the public transcript alone; the sender’s private context, reasoning, and tool calls stay unshared, and the draft distribution becomes a proxy for pt . The next section builds C ODETTA on this proxy.

5.2 Alphabet-Adaptive ECC 

From Section 5.1, the alphabet size changes from token to token. At every step, both parties compute Kt

from public information only, including the published transcript and the draft model, and we call a step with 

Kt > 1 usable. The sampler of Section 5.1 expects one symbol in ZKt at every usable step. The difficulty is that Kt is known only when generation reaches step t, by which time the symbols carrying the secret message of the previous t − 1 tokens are already embedded. A conventional ECC encodes the whole message into a codeword before transmission, so it cannot produce symbols in alphabets that are decided one step at a time. Running generation ahead to learn the sizes is not an option either, because the tokens that reveal Kt are the very tokens that carry the symbols, and once published they cannot be changed as rejecting and re-sampling a published token would alter the transcript distribution. A simple adaptation of a conventional ECC also falls short, because a binary ECC can pack a variable number of bits into each token only for alphabet sizes that are powers of two, so a step whose symbol alphabet has three, five, or seven possible values is rounded down to two or four, and the capacity in between is lost. We therefore design an alphabet-adaptive ECC that does not fix the codeword in advance. At each usable step it computes one symbol of the message in ZKt , so every error stays a substitution of one symbol and the number of symbols grows with the number of usable tokens. Algorithm 2 presents the complete protocol. 

Modular checks. Now we present the modular checks construction, which is at the heart of the alphabet-adaptive ECC that produces checks on the fly for the changing alphabet. A conventional ECC decides every symbol of the codeword before transmission, which is impossible when the alphabet changes at every token position. We take a different approach that instead of directly transmitting the message itself, the sender answers “questions” about the message, one “question” per usable token. The “questions” are public, so the receiver knows them as well. Each “question” is chosen at its own step, after Kt is computed by 

ChooseAlphabet , and it is designed to have exactly Kt possible answers, so the answer fits the alphabet of that usable token. For example, a token with Kt = 3 may carry the sum of two message bits modulo 3. The receiver collects the answers, a few of which can be wrong due to rejection in maximal coupling (see Section 4), and once it has enough such answers, it solves for the message similar to solving a system of equations with a few corrupted entries. There are several properties of such a construction to make it feasible for our setting. “Questions” can be posed for any number of answers, so the alphabet may change from token to token. “Questions” can be asked for as long as generation continues, so this construction becomes rateless. # 6 CODETTA Steganographic Key Exchange 

The protocols so far assume that the sender and the receiver share a key sk = ( km, kv). Independently deployed agents have no such key, especially if they are hosted by different organizations. They cannot establish the keys in the open, because a public key or a ciphertext in a conversation is an obvious signal to the auditor that they are transmitting secrets. The key exchange itself must therefore be hidden in ordinary text without a key. Hiding without a key is much more challenging than keyed scenarios. In the keyed protocols, the mask is a pseudorandom pad that the auditor cannot compute without the key, while the sender and the receiver, who both hold the key, compute the same pad and cancel it. The pad is uniform to the auditor, and Lemma 1 preserves the original distribution for every message. This construction does not generalize to the keyless setting. One keyless way to make a token carry a symbol is a public hash. The sender draws candidate blocks of tokens from the base distribution and publishes one whose hash equals the symbol, and the receiver recomputes the hash of the published block to read the symbol. Every party can compute the hash, so every message is public. The transcript keeps the base distribution only if the symbols are uniform, because otherwise the published blocks will be biased. A recent work follows this route [VZ26] and hides a key exchange whose messages are pseudorandom, so that every symbol is uniform by construction. The cost of this route lies in how the exchange is carried. The labels of a finite candidate pool are never perfectly balanced, and a sender that keeps the transcript distribution exact has to publish a block with the wrong label with probability equal to this imbalance, so a published symbol is wrong with a constant probability. The key exchange of [VZ26] tolerates such noise at the level of key agreement, and the feedback compiler in its Section 8 transmits the exchange bit by bit through a signaling protocol. Each published message carries a single bit of a key-exchange message, and the sender publishes a fixed number of messages for every bit, chosen in advance so that the receiver reads the bit correctly with high probability. Section 7.4 estimates the cost of their construction on our workloads and compares it with C ODETTA . The same work also proves two impossibility results [VZ26]. First, a pseudorandom code whose decoder is public cannot correct a constant fraction of bit flips; our carrier is not such a code, because the receiver decodes with its MLWE secret and no public decoding information exists. Second, a non-interactive key exchange with a pseudorandom transcript, in which the two messages are generated non-adaptively and every transcript bit is flipped independently with a constant probability, admits a quasi-polynomial attack. Our exchange satisfies neither condition. The sender’s response (w, z) depends on the share eb that it has read, so the messages are adaptive, and the carrier only displaces each q-ary coefficient by a small amount, whereas the result assumes that every transcript bit is flipped with a constant probability. CODETTA instead transports lattice coefficients directly through the carrier, without a separate bitwise signaling layer, and lets the MLWE absorb the carrier’s errors, and it certifies correctness on the workload itself (Theorem 7) instead of relying on the channel interface of their compiler, in which the sender knows 19 the crossover probability of each channel use before sending and learns afterwards whether the bit was flipped. The messages of MLWE key exchange are pseudorandom vectors of coefficients in Zq, and MLWE decryption tolerates small additive noise by design. We therefore send each coefficient as one q-ary symbol, about log 2 q ≈ 11 .7 bits per carrier position, and we allow noise in the carrier channel (see Section 6.4 for concrete parameters). The two designs thus take different directions from the same exact-distribution-preserving sampling. The construction of [VZ26] carries one bit per block and thereby works on short blocks of constant entropy, whereas C ODETTA carries a full q-ary symbol per position and therefore requires enough entropy in every block (Section 7.4 reports the failures on different workloads and models). A carrier error is a small displacement of a coefficient, which enters the exchange as extra noise and is absorbed together with the lattice noise, while a short ECC over the exchanged message corrects the residue. The carrier itself is exact. It publishes a block of text drawn from the base distribution at every position regardless of the symbol, so undetectability relies only on the pseudorandomness of the lattice messages. With the parameters of Section 6.4, a full exchange of a 128-bit key takes 1,280 carrier positions. In the following, Section 6.1 builds the carrier, Section 6.2 runs the exchange over it, Section 6.3 proves undetectability and correctness, and Section 6.4 instantiates the concrete parameters. 

6.1 Lifted Exact-Cover Carrier 

The carrier builds on the keyless hash-based embedding of the previous paragraphs. Its unit is one carrier position, in which one party publishes one block of tokens and thereby transports one coefficient a ∈ Zq of a lattice message; both messages of the exchange are sequences of such coefficients. We call the party that publishes the block the sender and the party that reads it the receiver; both agents take both roles during the exchange. The naive embedding has two weaknesses. First, a block is published with a probability that depends on how many other blocks share its label, so for a fixed pool the published block is not a uniform member of the pool and is honest only after averaging over the hash values. Second, when the pool has no block with the target label, the fallback delivers a random value to the receiver, making the coefficient’s additive noise large. The carrier removes both weaknesses. Its selection rule makes the published block a uniform member of the pool for every fixed pool and label table, so the block is an honest sample regardless of the labels. When the pool has no block with the target label, the rule publishes a block whose label is close to the target, so an error is a small displacement of the target label. 6.2 Key Exchange 

The exchange transports a random pre-key from one party to the other over the carrier of Section 6.1, and both parties derive the session key from the transmitted pre-key. We name the two parties by their roles in the keyed protocol that follows. The sender chooses the pre-key and later embeds messages under the resulting key, and the receiver recovers the pre-key and later extracts those messages. During the exchange, each party embeds the pseudorandom message into its own responses. The receiver publishes a pseudorandom share first, and the sender then publishes a vector and the masked pre-key, so the key exchange has three phases. Algorithm 4 shows the three phases with the sender on the left and the receiver on the right, and numbers the steps that the following paragraphs refer to. The key exchange should fit the carrier of Section 6.1, which restricts the messages it transports in two ways. First, every transported symbol must be uniform on Zq, since the published block is an honest sample only when the symbol is uniform (Lemma 2). Second, the exchange must tolerate a small error in every symbol, since the carrier can deliver a label near the target. A key transport based on MLWE satisfies both requirements by design. Under the MLWE assumption, every message of the transport is computationally indistinguishable from a uniform vector over Zq, so each of its coefficients can be handed to the carrier as a uniform q-ary symbol. The transport is also robust to small additive errors, because the receiver recovers the 22 payload by rounding each coefficient in the decoding to the nearer of two values that are q/2 apart, and an error that is small compared with this distance does not change the rounding. A displacement introduced by the carrier therefore has the same effect as the lattice noise that the transport already tolerates, and the two can be analyzed together. 6.3 Undetectability and Correctness of C ODETTA Steganographic Key Exchange 

The auditor of this section is passive. It reads every public message and may query the carrier hash and the KDF, which we model as random oracles, but it cannot modify, delay, inject, or choose messages. Since the two parties never see the auditor’s queries, an auditor that queries the oracles during the exchange has the same view as one that makes the same queries after the transcript is complete, so we analyze the latter.

6.4 Instantiation 

The proofs above hold for any choice of the lattice parameters, the serializers, and the ECC. This section fixes the choice used in the evaluation and states the security level it provides. The engineering parameters of the carrier, such as the replica ratios and the block length, do not enter the proofs, and Section 7 gives them with the experimental setup. 

Lattice parameters. We use the ring Rq with d = 256 and q = 3329, rank k = 2, χsec = CBD 3, and χerr =

CBD 2, where CBD η is the centered binomial distribution of parameter η. These are the parameters of ML-KEM-512 [Nat24]. The two assumptions of Theorem 5, decisional MLWE with k samples for the share and with k + 1 samples for the response, are the two assumptions in the IND-CPA security proof of ML-KEM-512, at the same ring, modulus, rank, and noise distributions [Nat24]. The exchange therefore relies on the same MLWE problems as ML-KEM-512, which NIST places in security category 1, at least as hard to break as exhaustive key search on AES-128. 

Serializers. The share b is sent with the identity serializer, as Lemma 3 requires. The response uses gw =

Compress 10 and gz = Compress 4, the ciphertext compression of ML-KEM-512, and the hard-decision map 27 HD is Compress 1. Compression to 10 bits changes a coefficient by at most q/211 ≈ 1.6 and compression to 4 bits by at most q/25 ≈ 104, and both are well below the decision threshold of q/4 ≈ 832 in Equation (23). 

Pre-key and ECC. The pre-key has ℓ = 128 bits, which matches the security category and makes the term 

QKDF /2ℓ in Equation (22) negligible at the same level, and the session key has κ = 256 bits. The ECC must place its codeword in the d = 256 coefficients of one ring element and carry at least ℓ information bits, and within these two constraints we take the largest correction radius. The binary BCH code of length 255 and dimension 131 of Section 2.4 corrects τ = 18 errors and meets both constraints. Its 131 information bits are the pre-key followed by three zero bits, and the unused coefficient of the ring element is set to zero. If the hard decisions were wrong independently with probability p per coefficient, this code would fail with probability at most 10 −6 for every p ≤ 0.0196. Section 7 reports the measured failure rates. The exchange occupies kd + kd + d = 1,280 carrier positions. 

# 7 Evaluation 

7.1 Experimental setup 

Tasks. We evaluate on three simulated agent workloads. F INANCIAL is a synthetic issuer-intelligence task with 20 issuers and 100 database records: the receiver agent asks about an issuer’s financial information, and the sender agent answers from its internal database. IFE VAL [ZLM +23] consists of natural-language requests with constraints on length, structure, wording, and formatting. CTF uses the offline InterCode-CTF [YPNY23] adaptation of picoCTF [CBB14]. For each CTF challenge, an agent first solves the challenge with shell and Python tools in a sandbox, and the carrier is the technical solution report that the same model then writes from its complete solve trajectory. These tasks simulate the realistic scenarios we have introduced in Section 1. In F INANCIAL and IFE VAL , the agents communicate in a conversational setup, and in CTF, the agents communicate by reading and writing to a shared file system. 

Asymmetry. The sender agent runs a large base model on its full private context; the receiver sees only public transcript, including its own request, the sender’s response, and public problem statement. On F INANCIAL ,the private context is the retrieved records. On IFE VAL , the sender first drafts a plan in its thinking trace, and that trace is the private context. On CTF, the private context is the agent’s full problem solving trajectory, including the task files, the tool commands, and the tool outputs. The receiver does not access the sender’s model, internal state, or private context. A draft-assisted receiver runs a small public draft model on the visible context alone; a model-free receiver does not run a language model. 

Models. For sender models, we evaluate on widely used open-sourced models, including Qwen2.5-7B [YYZ +24], Llama-3.1-8B [GDJ +24], and Ministral-3-8B [LKS +26]; the public draft models are Qwen2.5-1.5B, Llama-3.2-1B, and Ministral-3-3B. The model-mismatch ablation below pairs them across families, and the draft-size ablation additionally uses Qwen2.5-0.5B, 3B, and 7B as drafts. 

Baselines & Settings. ARS [BYP +25] is the state-of-the-art practical steganographic protocol under asym-metric resources, and we use its official implementation. We also construct a second baseline, HARQ LDPC, which replaces our alphabet-adaptive ECC with a rateless LDPC code: a 5G NR mother code of rate 0 .2whose parity bits are sent incrementally until the receiver decodes. We evaluate it in a draft-assisted and a draft-free configuration, using the optimized implementation in NVIDIA Sionna [HCAA +22]. CODETTA and the LDPC baselines carry 1,024 payload bits per message and append a 32-bit tag, so each message is 1,056 bits. The official ARS implementation delivers far fewer bits per token, so we give it a 128-bit payload on F INANCIAL and IFE VAL and a 16-bit payload on CTF; a shorter payload only helps ARS in a capacity comparison. We evaluate the average capacity of 25 independent secret messages with the same payload seed. A secret message spans several responses, i.e., the sender keeps embedding it across 28 consecutive responses until the receiver decodes it, and when a workload runs out of requests we rotate through its request pool again. Our primary metric is the end-to-end delivered payload rate (capacity) 

Rpayload = correctly recovered payload bits effective visible carrier tokens .

We also report encoding and decoding speed in tokens per second. We run the experiments on one NVIDIA H200 GPU. 

Ablations. To better understand the effectiveness of different components, we conduct three ablation studies. First, we vary information asymmetry by revealing 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, or 100% of the private context to the receiver. Second, we vary model mismatch across same-model, cross-size, and cross-family sender–draft pairs. Third, we remove one component of our code at a time by comparing with LDPC baselines: dropping the draft model removes public channel estimation, and fixing the alphabet size removes adaptive alphabet size selection, so the two variants separate the capacity gained from each. 

Key exchange. We run the key exchange of Section 6 with the three sender models on F INANCIAL , IFE VAL ,and CTF. Each of the m = 100 ,000 sessions replays the exchange algebraically, with fresh lattice randomness and with displacements drawn independently for every position from the distribution measured on the sender model’s candidate pools under eight independent session identifiers, so Theorem 7 certifies the failure probability under this measured displacement distribution. The carrier uses the first complete response block of a turn as its token block, capped at B = 64 tokens, and the replica ratios are (βb, βw, βz) = ( 4, 4, 1). The Compress 4 phase has only 16 labels, so one replica suffices there.

# 8 Related Work 

LLM steganography. Provably secure steganography goes back to the complexity-theoretic definition of Hopper, Langford, and Von Ahn [HLVA02] and the information-theoretic model of Cachin [Cac04], and Dedi´ c et al. showed that a sender who uses the channel only through sampling pays a number of samples exponential in the rate [DIRR05], which is the cost that our keyless carrier pays when it draws a pool of candidate blocks for every q-ary symbol. Language models made these definitions practical over linguistic channels. Ziegler et al. embedded bits with arithmetic coding over GPT-2 and measured the divergence 32 they introduced [ZDR19], and later constructions preserve the model distribution exactly or bound the distribution divergence: adaptive dynamic grouping [ZYYH21], Meteor [KJGR21], Discop [DCW +23], SparSamp [WPC +25], Shimmer [BPL +25], ANStega [WPC +26], range coding [YM26], and adaptive dy-namic sampling, which needs only a seeded sampling API [PB26]. All of them run in the symmetric setting: the receiver reruns the sender’s model on the same context, or queries the same model with the same seed, so that it can reproduce the sampling step, and independently deployed agents do not satisfy this assumption (Section 1). Mächtle et al. show that deterministic inference stacks leak a channel through the sampling seed, which the receiver recovers by exhaustive search over the seed space [MSB +26]. A model-free receiver appears in the asymmetric-resource protocol of Bai et al. [BYP +25], the ARS baseline of Section 7: the sender hides bits in permutations of the token distribution, and the receiver, which sees only the carrier, guesses the permutation through a sampling function; the protocol is robust over a binary symmetric channel, and our measurements place its rate below 0 .025 bits per token. Zamir hides an arbitrary payload in a response so that the response is indistinguishable without the key, extending the undetectable watermark of Christ, Gunn, and Zamir [Zam24], and Christ and Gunn build stateless steganography with constant rate and robustness to a constant rate of substitutions from pseudorandom codes [CG24]. These constructions are analyzed asymptotically. C ODETTA sits between the two extremes: its receiver runs a small public draft model using public context instead of the sender’s model and private states, and Section 7 measures the resulting gain in delivered payload rate. 

Steganographic key exchange. Von Ahn and Hopper introduced public-key steganography and stegano-graphic key exchange [VAH04]. As Vaikuntanathan and Zamir observe, the earlier covert key exchanges assume covertexts that are generated non-adaptively and a min-entropy per message that grows with the security parameter [VZ26]. Their own work is the closest to ours. With a shared key, they show that the tools of undetectable watermarking and steganography give a covert conversation whose rate matches the entropy of the honest messages up to constant factors. Without a key, they introduce pseudorandom noise-resilient key exchange, prove that covert key exchange is possible when sufficiently many honest messages have constant min-entropy, give constructions from sparse learning parity with noise and from a signaling compiler with noiseless feedback, and prove that public pseudorandom codes cannot correct a constant fraction of bit flips and that non-interactive protocols admit a quasi-polynomial attack. Section 6 compares the two key exchanges: theirs is a feasibility result without an implementation, whereas C ODETTA transports lattice coefficients directly over the carrier, without a separate bitwise signaling layer, and certifies correctness empirically on the measured channel, and Section 7.4 estimates the cost of their construction on our workloads. 

Watermarking and speculative decoding. Undetectable and distortion-free watermarks sample each token with randomness derived from a key so that the output distribution is unchanged, and their detectors need the key but not the model [CGZ24, KTHL24, CG24]. A watermark carries a fixed signal instead of a message, and the known limits of watermarking concern adversaries who edit or spoof the text [ZEF +24, PHZS24]. The maximal coupling of Section 4 is the acceptance rule of speculative decoding [LKM23, CBI +23], in which a small draft model proposes tokens and the target model accepts or resamples them so that the output keeps the target distribution exactly. C ODETTA applies the same rule to a bucket-conditioned proposal and treats a rejection as a channel error, while in speculative decoding, rejection reduces the decoding speedup. 

Collusion among LLM agents. A line of work studies collusion that emerges from the behavior of the model itself. Agents are prompted or trained to hide messages or reasoning in their text [MBS +24, MMM +25, RG23, ZM26], monitors of transcripts and chains of thought are designed and evaluated against agents that hide their intent [GSSR24, BHG +25, APB +26], and simulations and surveys map the risk of multi-agent collusion [RXW +25, Gha25]. These channels do not carry guarantees on the output distribution: the hidden content can change the model behaviors, so monitors and paraphrasers can detect or remove it. C ODETTA is instead a cryptographic protocol with a distribution-preservation guarantee of the output transcript, so the passive auditors cannot observe the hidden collusion messages by inspecting the communication transcripts. 33 9 Defense Discussion 

Theorem 3 and Theorem 5 show that a passive auditor cannot detect collusion from the transcript. In this section, we discuss three mitigation directions that go beyond transcript inspection. 

Auditing visible agent actions. The first direction is to audit the agents’ actions in addition to inspecting their messages. Coordinated actions (e.g., a trade or a tool call that the normal task does not require) are visible to the platform when they are executed, so an auditor on that platform can restrict the tools available to each agent, log every action, and check whether the actions are consistent with the assigned task. However, some actions may not be visible to the auditor. For example, sensitive data leakage is harmful as soon as the data leaves the sender, so the operator that deploys the sending agent can only limit the exposure in advance, i.e., give the agent access to no more data than its task requires. Data that the task legitimately requires, such as the retrieved records in F INANCIAL , remains exposed. We note that actions can also carry hidden information, e.g., through timing or through the choice among equivalent tool calls. 

Verifiable sampling. The second direction is to make the sampling itself auditable. The agent commits to its sampling randomness, e.g., derived from a public beacon, and proves in zero knowledge that every published token was sampled from its model’s distribution with that randomness, without revealing its private context [SLZ24, CWSK24]. C ODETTA embeds the hidden message by steering the sampling, so a valid proof rules out the covert channel. The drawback is cost: proving the inference of a large language model in zero knowledge is orders of magnitude more expensive than the inference in plaintext, so a future direction is to make this defense practical for deployment. 

Perturbing the communication channel. The third direction is to modify the messages before they reach the receiver, e.g., by paraphrasing each message or regenerating it with another model. Both protocols of CODETTA rely on the sender and the receiver seeing the same transcript. In the keyed protocol, the receiver derives the draft distribution, the alphabet size, and the bucket partition of every token from the public prefix (Section 5), so an edited token changes the symbols that the receiver reads at every later position, and in the key exchange an edit inside a block changes the label of that block. The error correction of C ODETTA is designed for the errors of the coupling, which leave the transcript intact, so a few edited tokens per message can already disable the channel. Robustness to such edits, e.g., through codes for insertion and deletion errors [CG24, MG24], is a natural extension of the design. Perturbations also have a cost of their own. They change the messages of honest agents, so their effect on task quality and latency must be measured for each deployment, and the component that rewrites the messages must be fully trusted by the auditor, since it sees and controls all of the communication. For example, the paraphraser must not embed secret messages of its own and must resist prompt injection attacks. We leave the design and evaluation of the mitigation to future work.

Example. Suppose one check involves only u1 and reads u1 = 1, and a second reads u1 ⊕ u2 = 1. The first check makes u1 believe it is 1 with probability 1 − e, where e is the channel error rate. In the next iteration u1

B.3 CTF 

CTF simulates two agents that communicate through a shared file system instead of a conversation. The sender agent first solves a challenge in an offline sandbox with shell and Python tools, in a ReAct loop of at most 50 messages and three flag submissions at temperature 1. The complete solve trajectory, i.e., the task statement with its attached files, every tool command, every tool output, and the submission feedback, is the sender’s private context. The same model then receives the report request below, appended to this trajectory, and writes one technical report on the task, which ends at its end-of-sequence token or after 576 tokens. The report is the carrier: the sender writes it into a directory that both agents can access, and the receiver agent reads the file from there, so the only communication between the two agents is the file itself. The receiver’s context is the public task statement and the report file, and it never sees the trajectory. The first 64 tokens of each report and all code blocks in it are generated without embedding, and the capacity in Table 1 divides the payload by all visible tokens of the report, including these. We have omitted the tool outputs in the example below.
