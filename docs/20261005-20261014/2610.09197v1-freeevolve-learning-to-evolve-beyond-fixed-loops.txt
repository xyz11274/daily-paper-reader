## ABSTRACT 

Agent evolvers automate the design of the prompts, skills and workflows around language model agents, yet the optimization process they follow is still designed by hand: a fixed search loop decides how candidates are evaluated, which are kept and when the search stops. We propose FREE EVOLVE , which automates this process as well. An environment specifies the goal, target agent, evaluator, data and resource limits; within these limits, the evolver itself decides what to test, how much evidence to collect, which candidates to pursue and when to stop. These decisions follow an editable evolution skill , which we improve through meta-evolution by scoring each candidate skill on the fresh target agent it produces. The optimization process thus becomes a capability learned from experience rather than a loop engineered in advance. On τ 3-bench, ARC-AGI-2, ARC-AGI-3 and Terminal-Bench 2.1, F REE EVOLVE controls the evolution campaign by itself, yet improves the primary held-out metric by 13.6 points on average and matches or exceeds hand-designed evolvers. The learned process keeps improving with experience: meta-evolved skills add 6.9 points over the seed skill on fresh target agents, demonstrating transferability across environments. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled agents to solve a wide range of tasks through reasoning and tool use. However, for a frozen LLM, the performance of an agent depends heavily on the harness around it. The harness determines how the model reasons, acts and recovers from failures. Therefore, improving the harness can improve agent performance without changing the model weights. Recent studies have started to automate this process by treating prompts, skills and agent workflows as editable components (Karten et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c). Following this direction, we focus on agent evolvers. An evolver executes a target agent, examines its failures and modifies its harness based on the collected evidence. Although evolvers automate the design of agent harnesses, the optimization process they follow is still designed by hand. Before evolution begins, a human-designed search loop fixes which tasks are evaluated, how often measurements are repeated, how candidates are compared and when the search stops. On one hand this requires extensive design expertise. On the other hand, a loop encodes assumptions about evaluation noise, task difficulty, useful curricula and available parallelism. These assumptions vary across agents and environments and can change as evidence arrives, yet an evolver that only proposes edits cannot act on that evidence, and adapting the loop means re-engineering it by hand. We therefore ask: Can the optimization process itself be automated, so that the evolver organizes its own improvement campaign and learns how to do so from experience?

To this end, we propose F REE EVOLVE , a framework that automates the optimization process of agent evolution. The main idea is to treat campaign organization as a capability that the evolver exercises and learns, rather than a loop that humans design. Our framework separates the improvement problem from the procedure used to solve it. A bounded environment defines the goal, editable target, available data and resource constraints. The evolver is invoked once and remains in control until it returns an improved target. After each environment execution, it uses the collected evidence to decide what to do next. This control allows the evolver to adapt the campaign without following an evolution loop defined in advance. 

Automating the process: campaign autonomy. Campaign autonomy allows the improvement process to adapt as new evidence becomes available. During evolution, the evolver can focus on tasks with frequent failures before validating more broadly. It can repeat uncertain comparisons, explore different candidates and reallocate resources based on the observed results. It can also stop when additional evidence is unlikely to change the best candidate. These decisions are made as evolution unfolds instead of being fixed in advance by a predefined search loop. 

Learning the process: meta-evolution. Campaign autonomy alone does not guarantee an effective improvement process. The evolver still needs guidance on how to use this control. To address this issue, we represent the campaign policy as an editable evolution skill . We then improve this skill through meta-evolution. Each candidate skill is used to run complete improvement campaigns in target-agent environments. The resulting agents and campaign traces are used to evaluate and refine the skill. Meta-evolution therefore produces a self-contained evolution skill that can be applied unchanged to new environments without retaining the original agents or execution artifacts. 

Experimental results. We evaluate F REE EVOLVE on τ 3-bench (Shi et al., 2026), ARC-AGI-2 (Chollet et al., 2026), ARC-AGI-3, and Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026). Without any hand-designed loop, meta-evolution raises held-out mean performance from 33 .3% to 51 .7% , 55 .4%

Installed on a fresh worker, the frozen meta-evolved skill outperforms the seed skill on all four mean metrics and all three available pass@3 metrics, and improves over a no-data LLM rewrite in all four environments, so execution experience, not instruction rewriting alone, drives the gain. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Automating harness design. Recent systems automate the design of the prompts, skills, code, and workflows around a fixed language model (Hu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Karten et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026; Lou et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026), and evolutionary coding systems improve programs and agent harnesses through model-generated edits (Sharma, 2025; Novikov et al., 2025; Agrawal et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). They automate what is edited; the search process that decides what to evaluate, which candidates to keep, and when to stop is typically specified in advance by the system designer. 

Automating the improver. Meta-evolution and self-improvement make the improvement mechanism itself editable, evolving mutation prompts, program improvers, self-editing agents, search strategies, context-producing skills, or meta-agents (Fernando et al., 2024; Zelikman et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Robeyns et al., 2025; Qu & Lu, 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b; Srikanth et al., 2026). Meta-Harness, does not do meta-evolution, it lets a coding proposer read candidate code, scores, and traces before editing (Lee et al., 2026). HyperAgents, like F REE EVOLVE , evolves at the meta level, jointly evolving task agents and the meta-agents that improve them, so its procedure can change across outer iterations (Zhang et al., 2026b). These prior work focus on improving a fixed campaign. 

Automating the improvement process. FREE EVOLVE extends automation to the process within a run: the evolver organizes its own campaign as results arrive, and meta-evolution learns the skill that guides it from completed campaigns. What is learned is neither a better artifact nor a search procedure for the next run, but a reusable way of running improvement campaigns. Appendix E discusses broader related work. 

## 3 METHOD 

In this section, we present the proposed F REE EVOLVE framework. Campaign autonomy changes who organizes the evolution process. In a conventional search loop, the framework invokes an evolver or proposer at predefined stages and retains control over evaluation, candidate selection and stopping. In F REE EVOLVE , the evolver is invoked once and remains in control until it returns an improved target. Briefly, the framework operates at two connected levels, as illustrated in Figure 2. At the agent level, a bounded environment defines the improvement goal, editable target, available data and resource constraints. The evolver follows an evolution skill and organizes the complete improvement campaign. It decides what to evaluate, how to allocate resources and when to stop. At the meta level, the evolution skill becomes the editable target. The meta-evolver evaluates candidate skills through complete agent-level campaigns and uses the collected evidence to improve the skill. Both levels use the same environment interface and evolution operation. 3Preprint. 3.1 A B OUNDED INTERFACE FOR CAMPAIGN AUTONOMY 

Giving control to the evolver requires a well-defined problem boundary. Within this boundary, the evolver can explore the problem in different ways. However, it cannot change the goal, evaluation rules or resource limits. We define an environment initialized with target x0 as 

E[x0] = ( g, D, x 0, R E , CE ) , (1) where g is the improvement goal, D is the available data and x0 is the initial editable target. The execution function RE runs a candidate target x under an execution request u and returns an artifact bundle a:

a = RE (x, u ). (2) The evolver chooses u, and CE defines which requests are valid. The execution function determines which interactions are possible; it does not prescribe when or in what order they are used. 

Worker environments. For an agentic benchmark, D is the evolution partition of the benchmark, 

x0 is the initial worker harness, and g is to improve that harness under the benchmark’s evaluation rules. RE executes a candidate harness and returns its scores, trajectories, and logs. The request u

makes execution choices part of the interface: the evolver may select tasks, set repetitions and seeds, and allocate concurrency and timeouts, subject to CE , which fixes the benchmark rules and bounds resources. In a prescribed loop these choices are made before evolution, so the evolver only ever sees 

RE (x) under a fixed u. Updating prior work to use u adaptively would require substantial domain knowledge and harness design expertise. 3.2 THE EVOLVER CONTROLS THE CAMPAIGN 

An evolver is an agent that receives this interface and runs the campaign. We instantiate it as Claude Code equipped with a self-contained evolution skill s, a markdown file of instructions. A complete campaign is one invocation: 

x⋆ = Evolve( s; E[x0]) ≡ s(g, D, x 0, R E , CE ) . (3) The framework invokes the evolver once, provides access to RE , and takes no further role. The evolver decides which candidate to edit, which request (x, u ) to issue, how the returned artifacts change its next decision, and when to return x⋆. No external loop fixes the number of executions, the organization of search, or the stopping decision. This is our operational definition of campaign autonomy: every environment execution is chosen by the evolver, and control returns to the evolver with the resulting artifacts. Two human-designed components remain, and neither is an evolution loop. Claude Code’s own agent loop (read, act, observe) is generic tool use; it specifies no evaluation schedule, acceptance rule, or stopping criterion. The seed skill is written by hand, but it fixes only dispositions: treat the environment as the source of truth, keep all evidence, continue from the best-supported target, and use the time budget (Appendix G.1). Which tasks to evaluate, how many repetitions to buy, when to branch, and when to accept are left to the evolver. 

Worker evolution. Let Ew[w0] denote a worker environment initialized with harness w0. Worker evolution is an ordinary application of Equation 3: 

w⋆ = Evolve ( s; Ew[w0]) . (4) Because the evolver initiates every execution, the campaign can follow the evidence: stable mea-surements let it move on, close comparisons justify more repetitions, and failures concentrated in part of the data can be searched there before a candidate is validated broadly. Whether a given skill organizes these choices well is a separate question, which we answer by learning the skill. 3.3 META -E VOLUTION LEARNS THE CAMPAIGN POLICY

Why the environment–evolver boundary matters. Separating environment assumptions from evolution strategy supports reuse across execution settings. On a cluster, inner campaigns finish asynchronously across nodes; an evolver tied to a single-machine execution model may leave resources idle or require changes to its control logic. In F REE EVOLVE , supporting Slurm requires only an environment-specific Rslurm m , which launches inner campaigns and returns their artifacts as they finish, and Cslurm m , which exposes node allocation and concurrency limits. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our central claim is that the policy organizing agent evolution can be learned rather than fully specified by a human-designed search loop. Campaign autonomy makes adaptive decisions available, and meta-evolution learns how to use them from experience. Our experiments answer five questions. 

Q1: Can a learned autonomous campaign achieve strong performance without a prescribed loop? 

Q2: Does meta-evolution turn campaign autonomy into a stronger evolver? Q3: Does the resulting evolution skill generalize beyond its source environment and model? Q4: How does the evolver use campaign autonomy as evolution unfolds? Q5: What model capability is required to exercise campaign autonomy effectively? Appendix Table 11 summarizes the evidence for each. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks and metrics. We evaluate four environments spanning interactive tool use, abstract reasoning, and terminal control: τ 3-bench for conversational agents over unstructured knowledge (Shi et al., 2026), ARC-AGI-2 for few-shot grid transformations (Chollet et al., 2026), ARC-AGI-3 for interactive abstract-reasoning games (ARC Prize Foundation, 2026), and Terminal-Bench 2.1 for long-horizon command-line tasks (Merrill et al., 2026). We report mean task score and pass@3, except on ARC-AGI-3, where we report RHAE. Appendix Table 5 lists dataset sizes, splits, metrics, and base agents. All meta-evolution campaigns start from the same task-agnostic seed skill; Appendix G.1 gives its instructions and the campaign decisions left open to meta-evolution. 

Evaluation protocol. We measure performance on unseen tasks rather than the best score seen during search. Each method searches only on the evolution partition, with the same split and worker configuration within a benchmark, and selects its final artifact using its own rules before the held-out partition is revealed. Following the standard search protocol used in GEPA and EvoX (Agrawal et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a), we perform the search once and additionally evaluate its resulting worker on the held-out set three times, reporting the mean and standard deviation (mean ± s.d.). Where defined, we also report pass@3, the fraction of tasks solved at least once across the three runs. 4.2 LEARNED AUTONOMOUS CAMPAIGNS PRODUCE STRONG WORKERS

To answer Q2 , every skill starts from the same fresh worker and receives the same resource budget. The seed is the initial evolution skill; the no-data variant asks an LLM to improve that skill without observing execution results; and the meta-evolved variant is learned from completed meta-evolution. No evolved worker or campaign artifact is carried into these runs. Figure 3 shows that the seed is already capable of improving a worker, but only modestly: it gains 0.6

points on average across the four mean metrics. The meta-evolved skill adds a further 6.9 points on average over the seed, improves every available pass@3 metric, and outperforms the no-data rewrite in every environment. Campaign autonomy makes a broad set of decisions available; these results show that meta-evolution can learn how to make those decisions effectively, reducing the need for manual expert design. 4.4 LEARNED EVOLUTION SKILLS TRANSFER ACROSS ENVIRONMENTS AND MODELS 

To answer Q3 , we test whether an evolution skill, which captures how to organize improvement rather than a particular worker, remains useful when installed elsewhere. If so, compute-intensive meta-evolution can be run once and the resulting evolver reused in new environments. We freeze each skill learned in a separate meta-evolution campaign and install it in a fresh target environment (Table 2). Transfer is positive on most target metrics, and some transferred skills approach or exceed the in-environment result. Both single-source skills, however, reduce the Terminal-Bench 2.1 mean, and the ARC-AGI-2 skill also reduces its pass@3, suggesting a transfer boundary when the target environment, including its hardware and execution behavior, changes substantially. To answer Q4 , we hold the learned skill and re-source budget fixed while removing its control over one environment-facing decision at a time. Table 4 examines how the learned skill uses its campaign control by fixing one decision at a time. Always evaluating the full set makes the evolver wait for expensive probes, reducing the number of decisions it can make within the time budget. Fixed concurrency and the absence of timeouts leave resources occupied by slow or failed executions, using more total tokens but producing a weaker worker. The benefit is thus not one universally optimal configuration; it is the ability to reorganize evaluation as the campaign unfolds. 

Qualitative campaign analysis. The fixed-decision interventions isolate evaluation choices, but autonomy also governs the organization of search. We therefore trace one ARC-AGI-2 worker-evolution campaign using a frozen meta-evolved skill. Figure 5 shows the evolver exercising this control online: it changes evaluation resolution, opens distinct branches, combines discoveries, and selects the final worker as evidence accumulates. These behaviors are not hard-coded stages of the framework; the evolver invokes each behavior when the campaign requires it. To identify what meta-evolution contributes to these decisions, we compare the four meta-evolved skills with their common seed. The seed provides broad dispositions—use the available time, preserve evidence, and continue from the best worker—while leaving the organization of the campaign open. The evolved skills turn those dispositions into operational policies (Appendix D, Figure 6). Across environments, the learned rules govern task selection, evaluation allocation, and search control. They use failures to choose new probes, move from cheap tests to confirmation only when warranted, and preserve or combine changes according to accumulated evidence. Together, the intervention, execution-trace, and skill analyses answer Q4 : autonomy lets the evolver request the campaign behavior it needs, while meta-evolution turns that freedom into a reusable way of organizing improvement. 9Preprint. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

Agent evolvers have automated the design of harnesses, but not the broader process that designs them. F REE EVOLVE automates that process too: the evolver runs its own improvement campaign in a free space, and meta-evolution learns how to run it from the outcomes. Across four environments, the automated process uses a self-controlled loop, yet matches or exceeds hand-designed evolvers’ performance. Beyond autonomy, the learned process improves with experience. What it carries is a way of running an improvement campaign rather than domain knowledge. Treating the optimization process as a learnable agent capability, rather than a fixed piece of engineering, lets agent improvement advance with experience and with the models themselves. 10 Preprint. ## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Anonymous code. We instantiate the evolver with Claude Code and a single instruction file, 

SKILL.md . One call to evolve.sh installs an environment in a fresh run directory and invokes this agent once. There is no framework-level evolution loop around that invocation. The seed skill contains only general instructions for working from evidence and leaving the strongest target behind; it does not prescribe an iteration count, branching structure, curriculum, evaluation schedule, or performance-based stopping rule. Each installed environment realizes Equation 1 through four visible components. 

ENVIRONMENT.md records the goal, data, constraints, and interpretation of the returned artifacts. The file .target identifies the editable surface x0, while run.sh implements RE . An initially empty workspace-<env>/ stores the candidates, artifacts, and notes produced during evolution. The environment files remain fixed throughout a campaign; only the declared target is editable. The call RE (x, u ) is implemented in place. The evolver installs candidate x at the path declared by 

.target and supplies u through the arguments of run.sh . Each call records its measurements and traces in an artifact directory. Evaluation always reads the declared edit surface, ensuring that the candidate under evaluation is the candidate being evolved. The run directory is the complete interface between environment and evolver. It is self-contained, records the skill and model used for the run, and preserves the evaluation artifacts produced during the campaign. The evolver may invoke run.sh in any order or configuration, launch independent branches, and decide when enough evidence has been collected. The framework enforces the environment boundary, but contributes no search schedule. The meta-environment uses exactly the same layout. Its declared target is a candidate SKILL.md ,and its run.sh launches that skill as the evolver in a fresh worker environment. A meta-level execution returns the complete inner run directory, including the worker produced, its evaluations, and the inner trace, rather than reducing the campaign to a fixed scalar. The outer evolver chooses which worker environment to launch and how many inner runs to collect. Meta-environments are excluded from the inner environment catalogue, bounding execution to the two levels described above. 

Hardware and campaign budgets. Standalone worker-evolution runs receive a 12-hour wall-clock budget on one 128-CPU machine. The environment permits at most 24 concurrent task attempts, regardless of how the evolver divides them among evaluation calls. Meta-evolution receives a 24-hour outer-campaign budget on a Slurm cluster with five worker nodes. The meta-environment submits each requested inner campaign as an independent one-node Slurm job, and Cm permits at most five inner campaigns to run simultaneously. We impose no separate wall-clock limit on an inner evolver within meta-evolution: deciding how many inner campaigns to launch, how long to let them run, and how to coordinate asynchronous completions is part of the meta-evolver’s campaign control.

Question Evidence 

Q1: Can a learned autonomous campaign achieve strong performance without a prescribed loop? 

FREE EVOLVE improves the primary held-out metric by 13 .6 points on average over the base worker, outperforms the expert-designed evolvers on three of four benchmarks, and matches or outperforms the meta-evolution baselines EvoX and HyperAgents. On Terminal-Bench 2.1 it has the best pass@3 but trails GEPA in mean score (Table 1). 

Q2: Does meta-evolution turn campaign autonomy into a stronger evolver? 

On fresh workers, the seed skill gains only 0.6 points on average across the four mean metrics. The frozen meta-evolved skill adds a further 6.9 points, improves every available pass@3 metric, and outperforms the no-data LLM rewrite in every environment (Figure 3). 

Q3: Does the resulting evolution skill generalize beyond its source environment and model? 

Frozen skills transfer positively on most target metrics, but both single-source skills reduce the Terminal-Bench 2.1 mean; joint meta-evolution on τ 3-bench and Terminal-Bench 2.1 improves every target metric over the seed (Table 2). Evolved worker harnesses also transfer across models: run unchanged with Sonnet 4.6 and Haiku 4.5, F REE EVOLVE keeps a 5.3-point average gain over the base harness (Figure 4a). 

Q4: How does the evolver use campaign autonomy as evolution unfolds? 

Fixing any single evaluation decision (always the full set, concurrency = 24 , or no timeout) yields a weaker worker, and the last two also use more tokens (Table 4). G.2 META -E VOLVED INSTRUCTION 

The meta-evolved version adds a more detailed experimental procedure. It instructs the learner to establish a baseline, diagnose failed trajectories, test different types of changes, account for noisy measurements, retain candidate versions, and restore the best-supported target before finishing.
