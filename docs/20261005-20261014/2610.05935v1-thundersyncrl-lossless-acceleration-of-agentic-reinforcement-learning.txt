Abstract 

Language models are moving beyond generating answers to pursuing long-horizon goals in interactive environments. Post-training these agents requires long, heterogeneous trajectories, and synchronous systems leave learner engines idle until rollout and verification finish. To squeeze out these pipeline bubbles, asynchronous training overlaps rollout and learning across updates, but comes at the cost of policy staleness. We introduce ThunderSyncRL , which starts gradient computation as soon as all required inputs are fixed, without policy staleness. For group relative policy optimization (GRPO), ThunderSyncRL computes each trajectory’s score gradient as soon as the reward for that trajectory arrives, without waiting for the group. For on-policy distillation (OPD), it computes gradients for each completed agentic turn’s teacher-scored actions while tool calls run in the sandbox. We prove that gradient streaming produces the same GRPO and OPD updates as batch-synchronous training, without changing either objective. On SWE-bench Verified and Terminal Bench 4.0, we train models to the same performance up to 1.9 × faster than synchronous training. With zero policy staleness, ThunderSyncRL also outperforms asynchronous training at a fixed budget by up to 2.47 percentage points. 0.0 64.0 128.0 192.0 256.0

1arXiv:2610.05935v1 [cs.LG] 5 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Language models are increasingly trained as agents for long-horizon decision-making in interactive environ-ments, reasoning and using tools in response to feedback. Reinforcement learning (RL) from environment or verifier feedback has emerged as a promising way to improve this behavior [JZY+25; FHQ+26; PWN+25; WDC+25; GZC+25]. However, training these agents requires interaction trajectories that are expensive to generate and heterogeneous in duration, with long-tailed completion times [HOC+26; CTL+26; RWL+26]. In batch-synchronous RL, learner engines remain idle until rollout engines finish the batch and all rewards arrive [FGS+25; RWL+26]. We found that learner GPUs sit idle for 49.5% of step time in synchronous GRPO and OPD training while they wait for rollout and verification (Figure 3). Prior work reports idle fractions as high as 81% [RWL+26], and the slowest 10% of rollout requests can take up to half of rollout time [QHH+26], which itself accounts for 70% to over 90% of training time [GZA+25; ZLS+25]. Existing systems reduce these pipeline bubbles, idle gaps between dependent stages, by overlapping computation and coordinating resource allocation [MFL+25; SZY+25; ZZW+25; FGS+25; HYW+26]. Asyn-chronous execution overlaps rollout and learning across updates, but some trajectories come from older policies. This policy staleness can require extra algorithmic machinery, including importance-weighted off-policy correction, filtering, clipping, or explicit staleness control [ESM+18; FGS+25; HYW+26; HLT+26]. On-policy pipelines overlap execution within a fixed rollout, but complete-group schedules still delay trajectory-local backward passes until all group rewards arrive [CHY+26]. We argue that the apparent trade-off between pipeline overlap and zero staleness is artifactual, arising from coarse execution barriers. Although a synchronous update requires the complete batch, its gradient computation can begin earlier. For example, in group relative policy optimization (GRPO) [SWZ+24], group rewards determine how to weight each trajectory’s score gradient, but the gradient can be computed before all rewards arrive. We therefore ask: can learner computation begin as soon as its objective dependencies are satisfied with zero staleness? 

We introduce ThunderSyncRL , which streams gradients through dependency-aware scheduling of learner work. During update k, trajectories are sampled from πθk and gradients are evaluated at θk. For GRPO, a trajectory’s advantage seems to need every reward in its group. ThunderSyncRL nevertheless starts that trajectory’s backward pass as soon as its own reward arrives, and two running sums of score gradients recover the exact group gradient once the group closes (Section 3.4). For on-policy distillation (OPD) [AVZ+24], ThunderSyncRL releases a turn’s student backward pass once a frozen teacher scores the turn’s action tokens, allowing overlap with tool execution and later turns. After all trajectories, groups, and gradient work finish, ThunderSyncRL combines the gradients, takes one optimizer step, and publishes θk+1. ThunderSyncRL is therefore asynchronous in execution and synchronous in policy updates. We use SWE-smith and SETA-Env for post-training [YLJ+25; SHK+26] and evaluate on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal Bench 4.0 [JYW+24; MSC+26]. Our scheduling analysis predicts step wall time speedup approaching 2 × over synchronous execution when rollout and learner work are balanced and the residual tail and publication cost vanish. With a fixed trajectory batch size, shorter steps let ThunderSyncRL learn from more trajectories within the same GPU-hours, potentially improving end-to-end performance. Figure 1 compares ThunderSyncRL with Sync by training cost: each arrow marks where ThunderSyncRL reaches the pass@3 of a Sync checkpoint, at 1.6 to 1.9 × lower cost. # 2 Related Work 

2.1 Post-training with Reinforcement Learning Towards Agentic Tasks 

RL post-training extends single-turn generation to agents that learn through long-horizon interaction with ex-ternal environments. ArCHer develops hierarchical multi-turn RL, while DigiRL combines offline and online RL for mobile-device control [ZZP+24; BZC+24]. Web and search agents learn through self-evolving curricula, browser interaction, and outcome rewards [QLI+25; WYL+25; JZY+25; SJM+25; CSL+25; ZFH+25]. Tool-use research studies strategic interpreter use, fine-grained rewards, planning supervision, and dynamic user inter-action [FHQ+26; QAH+25; LHW25; ZWM+25]. Further work addresses trajectory-level optimization failures, multi-step supervision, and procedural or capability-targeted training environments [WWW+25; GMZ+25; DLH+26; SHK25; KSS+26]. Software agents learn from executable environments, software-evolution data, and execution feedback [PWN+25; WDC+25; GZC+25]. Agent Lightning and VerlTool provide reusable infrastructure across heterogeneous agent workflows [LZH+25; JLL+26]. 

2.2 Optimization of Reinforcement Learning 

Distributed RLHF systems coordinate generation, reference and reward inference, and actor and critic training, using model placement, device reallocation, and stage fusion to reduce idle time [YAR+23; HWS+25; MFL+25; SZY+25; ZZW+25]. Building on actor-learner decoupling in distributed RL [ESM+18; PHK+20], LlamaRL, AReaL, AsyncFlow, and StreamRL support disaggregated generation and training, streaming exe-cution, asynchronous weight synchronization, and control of policy staleness [WWT+25; FGS+25; HYW+26; ZZS+25]. For heterogeneous agentic rollouts, PipelineRL and RolloutPipe pipeline rollout and learner execution [PKP+25; CHY+26]. JigsawRL, Libra, TideRL, and WAR address fine-grained scheduling, elastic resource allocation, long tails, and inference caching [HOC+26; CTL+26; RWL+26; XZB+26]. SAO performs asynchronous optimization from individually completed rollouts [HLT+26]. # 3 Methods 

We present ThunderSyncRL, a gradient streaming framework for zero-stale agentic post-training (Figure 2). ThunderSyncRL releases learner computation when each source becomes ready . Exact gradient reduction and a synchronous optimizer barrier preserve the update semantics of GRPO and on-policy distillation (OPD) while allowing rollout and learning to overlap. 

3.1 Background 

Group relative policy optimization (GRPO). Given a prompt, GRPO samples a group of trajectories and normalizes their rewards within the group to obtain relative advantages. The policy update weights each trajectory’s scored action-token log probabilities by its detached advantage. 

On-policy distillation (OPD). OPD generates trajectories with the student policy and evaluates the student’s action tokens under a frozen teacher. The resulting token-level supervision defines a distillation loss for the action tokens of each agentic turn. 

Actor-learner execution protocols. Agentic reinforcement learning alternates between two expensive stages: generating long, tool-using trajectories and optimizing the policy from the resulting training signals. Conventional synchronous training places a batch barrier between these stages, leaving the learner idle until every trajectory and reward is available (Figure 3). Fully asynchronous methods remove this idle time, but typically update the policy while older rollouts remain in flight, introducing policy staleness. ThunderSyncRL follows a single principle: each computation is released at the earliest time when its mathematical dependencies are satisfied. 43.2 Problem Formulation 

Objective sources. Consider update k with policy πθk and logical rollout batch Bk. Each trajectory is a sequence of agentic turns, where a turn contains a policy-generated action and the resulting environment or tool observation. We call an independently releasable unit of learner computation an objective source . Source 

j is ready at time τj once all its inputs are fixed. 

Release semantics. The ready unit depends on the training objective. For a GRPO group G of m trajectories, source i is associated with trajectory i and becomes ready when its token sequence, scored-token mask, and reward ri are fixed. The group statistics remain unavailable until all m rewards arrive. In OPD, the scored action tokens of each turn form a source. Once these tokens and their mask are fixed, the frozen teacher can score them using their realized causal context. The teacher scores release the corresponding student backward pass, which can overlap with tool execution and subsequent turns. 

Scheduling objective. Our problem is to release each source at its earliest objective-valid time while preserving the corresponding batch-synchronous update. All trajectories in update k are sampled from πθk ,and all gradients are evaluated at the fixed parameters θk. After the logical batch closes and all gradient work finishes, the optimizer takes one step to produce θk+1. The logical batch and objective remain unchanged; only the timing of eligible learner work differs. 

3.3 ThunderSyncRL Framework 

Release rule. ThunderSyncRL releases each source’s learner computation at its ready time τj, which can precede the completion of its group or of the batch; gradients accumulate at θk.

Readiness-driven scheduling. Ready sources enter a work-conserving queue in readiness order; admission never waits for a smaller trajectory index, or for an unrelated group below a per-rank cap (Appendix D). Independent groups may be reduced concurrently, while each group preserves a deterministic reduction order. We include deferred gradient propagation at trajectory or group closure as learner work with its own release time. Index learner work items in service order; let τj and pj denote the release time and service time of item j. On a single learner lane with c0 = 0, completion time satisfies 

cj = max {τj, cj−1} + pj. (1) For n learner work items, let Rk be the time at which the complete rollout batch closes and Pk the common final reduction, optimizer, and publication suffix. The update endpoint is 

Tk = max {Rk, cn} + Pk. (2) A batch-synchronous method postpones all learner work until Rk. For GRPO, a group-ready pipeline releases all sources in a group at its final reward. ThunderSyncRL uses each source’s ready time for either objective, moving eligible learner work into rollout intervals and reducing the learner tail after the final trajectory. 

Zero-stale optimizer barrier. ThunderSyncRL waits for every registered trajectory and group to close and for all deferred gradient propagation to finish. Distributed reduction and objective-specific normalization then produce the batch update direction gk (Section 3.4). ThunderSyncRL clips this direction and applies exactly one optimizer step, 

θk+1 = Opt (θk, clip (gk)) . (3) ThunderSyncRL publishes the resulting adapter or policy parameters to the rollout replicas. Each replica loads the new version, clears policy-dependent state, and acknowledges that it serves θk+1 before the next update begins. Every rollout and learner source within update k is therefore bound to θk, and no trajectory is optimized against a policy that changed while it was in flight. 4.1 Setup 

Models, datasets and benchmarks. 

• Models: We use Qwen-3.8-27B [Qwe26a; Qwe26b] for full fine-tuning and the separate LoRA config-uration (Appendix H.2). The model comparison also includes GLM-4.7-Flash-31B [Zai26; GZL+25]. Appendix D specifies the Qwen OPD teacher, which remains fixed across execution schedules. • Datasets: We use SWE-smith [YLJ+25] and SETA-Env [SHK+26] for post-training, covering repository-level software engineering and broader terminal tasks, respectively. Appendix C details data revisions, filtering, splits, and benchmark separation. • Benchmarks: We evaluate on SWE-bench Verified [JYW+24] and Terminal Bench 4.0 [MSC+26], using the same tasks, agent interface, and inference budget across execution schedules. Terminal Bench 4.0 tasks are excluded from post-training. Appendix E specifies success criteria, pass@3 aggregation, and checkpoint comparisons. We also report pass@1 and pass@5 on both benchmarks in Appendix H.1. 

Workloads and configurations. Each GRPO update uses 32 prompts with eight trajectories sampled per prompt, totaling 256 trajectories. Each OPD update uses 32 prompts with four independent trajectories sampled per prompt, totaling 128 trajectories. Both objectives allow up to 300 agentic turns and 1,024 generated tokens per turn. The reference configuration uses a single node with eight NVIDIA B200 GPUs; the hardware comparison also includes a node with eight NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Appendix D provides the full optimization and generation settings. 

Baselines. Within each objective, we hold the model, workload, optimizer, precision, and hardware allocation fixed and compare ThunderSyncRL against three execution schedules: • Sync: A zero-stale baseline that starts learning after the complete rollout batch is available. • Async: A one-update-stale baseline that overlaps learning with the next rollout batch. • Fully Async: Rollout and learning proceed without a batch-level barrier between them. Policy updates can occur during in-flight trajectories, yielding variable policy lag. Sync and Async use the official verl [SZY+25] pipeline for GRPO and the SkyRL [CHL+25] pipeline for OPD. Sync, Async, and ThunderSyncRL use vLLM for rollout, with the same chunked prefill and prefix caching settings. 4.3 Efficiency across models and hardware 

The speedup follows how the stages balance on each model and GPU (Section 4.4). On B200, Qwen-3.8-27B is near balance and reaches 1.91 × and 1.94 × over Sync under GRPO and OPD. GLM-4.7-Flash-31B activates about 3B parameters, so its learner work is short, rollout bounds the step, and the speedup is 1.64 × and 1.63 × (Table 1). # 5 Conclusion 

We presented ThunderSyncRL, which overlaps rollout and learning with zero off-policy staleness. It streams ready gradients at a fixed policy snapshot and takes one optimizer step after the batch closes, preserving the synchronous GRPO and OPD updates. Gradient sufficient statistics let GRPO backward passes start before all group rewards arrive, and teacher-scored turns let OPD learning proceed during tool execution. Step wall time speedup can approach 2 × when rollout and learner work are balanced and the learner tail and update overhead are negligible. Agent training at frontier scale moves only as fast as experiments turn into evidence. By recovering idle learner time without changing the objective, ThunderSyncRL shortens that loop, so the same compute answers more research questions. ThunderSyncRL rests on one premise: learner work should begin as soon as the objective’s dependencies allow. How much this saves is therefore set as much by the workload as by the scheduler. Objectives whose gradient inputs resolve early, such as turn-level OPD, leave more learner work to overlap with rollout than objectives that wait on group-level signals, and the benefit is largest when rollout and learner work take comparable time. Starting work early also means keeping partial gradient state until each group or batch closes, which exchanges trainer memory for wall time. We regard these dependencies as a design space. Finer readiness units and admission that weighs memory against remaining service would move more learner work into the rollout window, and identifying readiness units for other objectives would extend exact gradient streaming beyond GRPO and OPD. Scaling the same principle to multi-node training and a broader set of agentic workloads is a natural next step.

B.4 Hardware allocation and memory 

Each eight-GPU configuration assigns four GPUs to the learner. GRPO uses the remaining four for rollout; OPD uses two for rollout and two for the teacher. ThunderSyncRL replicates parameters, shards optimizer state, and accumulates gradients on the host. Appendix D gives the precision and execution settings. The dense-BF16 peak rates used in the MFU denominator are 2,250 TFLOP/s per B200 GPU and 989.5 TFLOP/s per H200 GPU. We report two kinds of memory. GPU memory is the HBM on each learner GPU; host memory is the CPU RAM of the node. Tables 1 and 2 report trainer peak GPU memory per GPU. The reported peak is the largest among the learner GPUs during an update, including gradient accumulation, distributed reduction, and optimizer execution. It covers resident model and optimizer state, activations, gradient buffers, and temporary storage on those GPUs. Rollout KV caches and teacher memory belong to their separate GPU pools. 21 C.3 Scope of benchmark separation 

Excluding benchmark task identifiers is only one check on data separation. The SWE-smith paper describes using a SWE-bench Verified problem statement as a style demonstration during issue generation (Appendix D of [YLJ+25]). Repository disjointness therefore does not establish that its construction was independent of benchmark text. For SETA, checks must cover instructions, reference solutions, tests, and source ancestry, including relationships to earlier Terminal Bench releases. The static checks above do not establish semantic decontamination. The overlap review first matches task IDs, canonical repository or source IDs, and hashes of whitespace-normalized instructions, solutions, and tests. Candidate paraphrases and evolved descendants then require source inspection. A confirmed benchmark derivative excludes its entire source family before splitting; ambiguous cases remain recorded rather than being certified clean. Counts of candidates, confirmed overlaps, and exclusions accompany the final cohort manifest. The protocol excludes benchmark tasks, solutions, verifier outputs, and identified derivatives from training, prompts, and development selection. Upstream data generation or model pretraining may still expose the student or teacher to benchmark content. Results therefore compare schedules with matched data and models, without establishing that every task was unseen throughout model development.

D.4 Comparison controls 

Comparisons fix the objective, model, adaptation, initial checkpoint, training list, rewards, optimizer, precision, serving settings, environment resources, and hardware across schedules. GRPO and OPD differ in trajectory counts and supervision costs, so equal updates do not imply equal compute. Sync and ThunderSyncRL use one policy snapshot for rollout and learning. Async follows a one-step-off schedule: after startup, each training batch is sampled from a policy one optimizer update behind the current learner. Specifically, trajectories are sampled from πθk−1 , while the learner evaluates the same surrogate at θk, without an additional behavior-policy importance correction. Holding the surrogate fixed makes this a controlled comparison of execution schedules rather than of staleness corrections such as the decoupled objective of AReaL [FGS+25]. Fully Async follows the fully asynchronous trainers of verl [SZY+25] for GRPO and SkyRL [CHL+25] for OPD, in the style of AReaL [FGS+25]. Rollout and learner GPUs run independently: the learner starts an update once the completed groups for one logical batch are available, while rollout continues. After each update, the new weights reach the rollout engines in flight: generation pauses, the weights are swapped, and unfinished trajectories resume under the new policy. A trajectory can therefore span several policy versions, and one batch can mix trajectories of different ages. A staleness bound limits how far rollout runs ahead of the learner, so the policy lag varies but stays bounded. For Sync, Async, and ThunderSyncRL, completion order affects admission, not batch membership: every scheduled trajectory and scored turn completes before the update. The shared-prefix learner optimization in Appendix I also applies to all schedules. Equal mathematical updates need not be bitwise identical across kernels.

Shared-prefix backward in all schedules. Sync, Async, and ThunderSyncRL all reuse shared-prefix compu-tation on the learner. Training-time prefix reuse has earlier applications in preference optimization [WH24] and in branching agent trajectories [WWC+26]. At a fixed learner snapshot, we compute the shared prefix once, process each continuation with its causal context, and accumulate its gradient with respect to the prefix boundary states. After all branch contributions are available, one backward pass through the shared prefix propagates their sum, with the objective’s weights and normalization preserved. For GRPO, the reward-weighted and unweighted boundary contributions are combined when the group rewards are fixed. For OPD, teacher-scored turns contribute boundary gradients before the final token normalization. This 37 is a common learner optimization in the comparison, distinct from vLLM’s inference-time prefix cache. ThunderSyncRL admits eligible branch work earlier; shared-prefix backward remains part of each schedule’s measured learner work and update time. The following Python-style pseudocode describes one logical update of ThunderSyncRL. Rollout workers and the learner use the same fixed policy version throughout the update. Each learner rank processes its assigned groups. Helpers represent model execution, gradient storage, and distributed collectives.
