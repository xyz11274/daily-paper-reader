# EliteTable: Self-Improving Program Search for Executable Control Policies 

## Stuart Farmer 

Casa Dilauro Independent Research (casadilauro.com) 

stuart@casadilauro.com github.com/StuartFarmer/elitetable 

Abstract —EliteTable is a self-improving program-search algorithm guided by large language models (LLMs) that uses a fixed-capacity table of high-scoring solutions to guide new proposals, edits of existing solutions, and remixes of several solutions. Performance was evaluated across six Gymnasium control tasks, each with ten generations and fifty candidate slots per generation. Each task was evaluated across ten independently initialized searches. Candidate-score medians rose between the first and final generations in all six tasks, and selected-winner search-score medians rose in five. In CartPole-v1 , the selected winner reached the search-score ceiling in the first generation of all ten searches. All ten paired held-out endpoints improved in four of the six tasks. BipedalWalker-v3 and CarRacing-v3 improved without reaching their reporting targets. Six of ten Acrobot-v1 searches declined on held-out episodes, and the median paired change was negative. The results document improvement during program search and the transfer of selected-winner gains to new episodes in some tasks. Comparisons and ablations are needed to establish EliteTable’s relative performance and the contributions of its components. 

## 1 Introduction 

This work was motivated by the broader study of recursive self-improvement and LLM-guided program search: how these algorithms are structured, which tasks they address, how well their solutions generalize, and which mechanisms support improvement. The study examines one part of that motivation: whether a program-search algorithm inspired by prior work can also show improvement through repeated eval-uation and revision. In program search, one part of the improvement loop proposes a solution, often source code or an executable policy. Another part, the evaluator, provides a score or feedback on how that solution performs on the task. 

Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) illustrates this feedback pattern: a language model receives a task de-scription and previously evaluated solutions, then uses that feedback to propose further solutions [1]. 

FunSearch makes this feedback loop concrete for ex-ecutable programs. It uses an island-based database of solutions, maintaining multiple populations to support diversity. Its applications include cap-set constructions and online bin-packing heuristics [2]. 

AlphaEvolve extends evolutionary code modification to scientific and computational problems, using evalu-ator feedback to guide revisions [3]. In these systems, evaluation gives program search an external measure of whether a proposed change is useful. These algorithms differ in what they retain and how they generate candidates. ShinkaEvolve combines par-ent sampling, code-novelty rejection, and bandit-based selection among language models [4]. 

Evolution of Heuristics evolves descriptions of heuristic ideas together with their executable implementations, giving the language model both conceptual and code-level information about the solutions it explores [5]. EliteTable draws on these approaches through a fixed-capacity table of high-scoring programs. New candidates compete with retained solutions after each generation, while three operators generate fresh proposals, edits, and remixes. The design aims to provide a simple feedback loop for repeated program improvement. Keeping incumbents eligible guarantees that the best recorded search score cannot decrease, but does not guar-antee that newly generated solutions improve. A search may retain elites based on similar heuristics and become confined to a local optimum [2]. Selected solutions may also fail to generalize to new episodes, so held-out evalu-ation is necessary [10]. The study asks two questions. First, do in-sample winner scores and candidate-score distributions improve across generations? Second, do selected-winner gains transfer to held-out episodes? I examine these questions across six Gymnasium control tasks [7]. Records were kept for every candidate across the sixty searches. Between generations one and ten, pooled candidate-score medians increased in all six tasks and median selected-winner search scores increased in five.

2.1 Generation and repair 

EliteTable uses three operators: new, edit, and remix. Generation one contains only fresh proposals; the top 

K evaluated solutions enter the elite table. Subsequent generations use the allocation in Table 2 . Fresh proposals receive elite descriptions and scores, edits receive one parent’s source, and remixes combine information from up to three parents. Parents are sampled uniformly from the table frozen at the start of the generation. Before evaluation, proposed solutions are checked for schema compliance, valid source edits, and parseable Python syntax using the abstract syntax tree (AST). Solutions must satisfy the required policy interface and pass duplicate checks. Duplicate detection compares AST structures rather than source text. Solutions are then passed to the evaluator. 2.2 Elite selection 

As briefly mentioned, EliteTable retains the highest-scoring programs across completed generations. For elite capacity K, the update rule is 

Et = TopK ( Et−1 ∪ C t) . (1) Here Et is the retained elite set after generation t, Ct

contains valid new candidates from that generation, and TopK selects up to K programs. Candidates are ranked by descending mean search reward. For equal scores, edits take priority over remixes, fol-lowed by incumbent elites and then fresh proposals. Any remaining ties are resolved by ascending organism identi-fier. This persistent elitist mechanism ensures that the high-est recorded search-panel reward cannot decrease between completed generations, assuming previously recorded eval-uations remain unchanged. This monotonicity does not imply monotonic held-out performance.

3.2 Environments and policy interface 

Generated solutions must implement Solution(Policy) ,a Python class with an asynchronous action method. Policy objects receive the current observation, observation and action spaces, and task instructions. The policy interacts with the environment through an observe–act loop. Its runtime interface does not expose reward values or environment internals. A policy may retain state within an episode, provided it satisfies the required interface. That state must be reset at the start of each episode. Generation and repair prompts include task docu-mentation, resolved environment settings, the observa-tion/action contract, the objective, and execution con-straints. This documented task knowledge is distinct from the information exposed to a policy during execution. For BipedalWalker-v3 , the prompts also include ex-plicit observation indexing, normalization, motor order-ing, and reward details. Prompt construction is config-urable by task. The study therefore evaluates program search with documented task knowledge, rather than learning solely from interaction. 

3.3 Model configuration 

I used the requested model route openai/gpt-oss-120b: nitro through OpenRouter for proposal generation and repair. Responses were capped at 16,384 tokens per request. The runner does not explicitly set temperature or top-

p, so the study does not specify fixed values for those parameters. Returned model, provider, and usage meta-data are retained when available. The requested route does not guarantee identical provider execution across requests. Because fresh proposals receive elite summaries, they cannot serve as a history-independent sampling baseline.

3.5 Execution and reproducibility 

Sixty searches were completed: ten initialization seeds across six tasks. Each began at generation zero with no existing solutions. Evaluation ran on an AMD64 host using eight worker processes, with up to 25 concurrent model operations for generation and repair. The config-ured deadlines were 45 seconds per episode, ten seconds per policy action, and 120 seconds per model-operation attempt. The episode and action deadlines exclude Docker startup, queueing, and final cleanup. Generated poli-cies ran in Docker containers with networking disabled, a read-only root filesystem, and resource limits. Core reproduction code and recorded evidence are avail-able at https://github.com/StuartFarmer/elitetable. The accompanying reproduction guide identifies the pinned revision and offline reconstruction commands. The retrospective sign-test extension and additional in-puts and scripts for supplemental analyses accompany this manuscript locally; they are not included in that pinned public release. Reconstructing recorded results and running new stochastic searches are distinct proce-dures. 

## 4 Experimental Results 

4.1 Completion and improvement during search 

All sixty searches completed. Held-out measurements were available for 599 of 600 generation-winner panels. The missing MountainCar-v0 search-seed-8 panel was at generation six and did not affect any endpoint pair. Median selected-winner search scores increased be-tween the first and final generations in five environments. 

CartPole-v1 winners were already at the score ceiling in generation one. Newly evaluated candidate medians rose between gener-ations one and ten in all six tasks, including CartPole-v1 .

Figure 2 shows these population changes, extending the evidence beyond retention of strong initial winners. These distributions contain newly evaluated candidates, not retained elites or held-out winners. Each score aver-ages ten search episodes; failed or discarded candidates without scores are excluded. The LunarLander-v3 dis-play is restricted to returns between −1000 and 350: 198 scores outside this range remain in the calculations. Candidate-score distributions show bimodality in some tasks. ## 5 Discussion and Conclusion 

The rising candidate-score medians show progress beyond retaining a single strong initial solution. Newly generated populations also shift toward higher returns. In four tasks, every final selected winner improved over its first-generation counterpart on the fixed held-out episode panel. These endpoint gains do not by themselves rule out overfitting to the selection panel. 

Acrobot-v1 exposes a limitation: search scores rose while the median paired held-out change was negative. Further examination is needed to identify the cause. Held-out evaluation covers each generation’s selected winner, not every retained elite, so it does not describe the full elite population. Search used ten episode seeds, compared with 100 held-out seeds. Future studies could test whether broader selection panels reduce specialization; the current design does not isolate that effect. 

BipedalWalker-v3 and CarRacing-v3 improved with-out reaching their reporting targets. The tested budget does not establish whether additional generations would eventually solve these tasks. Their gains should therefore be distinguished from task attainment. 

5.1 Program search and neural-policy training 

EliteTable revises policy source code rather than up-dating neural-policy weights through policy gradients. Source edits can change numerical constants as well as program structure. Policy-gradient methods instead up-date parameterized policies, often neural networks, using estimates of the gradient of expected return with respect to policy parameters [8]. The selected tasks are drawn from Gymnasium’s stan-dard reinforcement-learning environments [7]. Matched comparisons are needed to assess the relative strengths of program search and neural-policy training on these tasks. Source revisions can alter control structures, numeri-cal constants, state, and execution flow. Programmatic reinforcement learning can also combine program search with guidance from a trained neural policy [9]. These are different update mechanisms; the present study provides no matched comparison of their learning efficiency. 65.2 Limitations and next steps 

Understanding EliteTable’s components requires abla-tions and comparisons with other systems. The observed improvements cannot yet be attributed specifically to reuse, editing, remixing, retention, or repair. Ten searches per environment quantify variation under fixed episode panels, but do not establish robustness across episode distributions, language models, prompts, or search con-figurations. The tasks are well known, so the model may have encountered descriptions or potential solutions during training. Time-based deadlines can produce different outcomes on different hardware. Hosted-provider variation can also change model outputs and search trajectories, even when the same model route is requested. The final solutions are Python programs available for inspection, but source availability alone does not establish human interpretability. I would prioritize budget-matched comparisons with other program-search algorithms, including a history-independent generation baseline, followed by complete-search ablations and comparisons across language models. Longer budgets, new episode panels, and other task fami-lies would test the limits of the observations. The present results provide development evidence for those designs rather than confirmatory evidence of comparative superi-ority. 

5.3 Conclusion 

EliteTable contributes to the study of self-improving LLM-assisted program search. Across sixty searches, candidate populations improved on selection episodes, while held-out evaluation distinguished transferable gains, saturation, and regression. This work supports further investigation of repeated program improvement as part of the broader agenda toward recursive self-improvement. It does not test a system that modifies its own optimizer or language model.
