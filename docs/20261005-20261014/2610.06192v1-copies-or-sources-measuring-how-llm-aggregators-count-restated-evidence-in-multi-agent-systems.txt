## Abstract 

Multi-agent systems built on large language models (LLMs) restate observations as a matter of course: relays forward them, shared boards repeat them and discussion rounds echo them. An aggre-gator that pools such messages should count sources, not state-ments. We convert a reported probability into units of independent readings, which assigns every restatement a copy weight, 0 for an aggregator that counts sources and 1 for one that counts every statement, and yields the implied decision under any cost structure. Three testbeds hold the evidence fixed and vary how it is restated: message logs with an exact Bayesian oracle, web documents with appended copies, and logs written by LLM agent teams under four communication protocols. Across four models from three providers, a forwarded copy counts for 0.06 to 0.42 of a new reading, mostly because some replies count every statement. On 5% to 40% of logs that state one reading three times, the reported belief implies an early commitment that the oracle never makes. The models that count copies least and most on controlled logs do so on web copies and agent-written logs as well. A one-paragraph declaration of what a copy contributes brings the copy weight on controlled logs to 0.08 or less. A rule that has agents refer to readings instead of restating them cuts belief-implied early commitment from 11.2% to 1.1% and preserves genuine corroboration.

## 1 Introduction 

A rover team must decide whether a dust storm will reach its land-ing site before it commits to a manoeuvre. Agent A1 reads sen-sor S1 and reports storm, and agents A2 and A3 pass the report on. The team’s log now holds three storm messages but a sin-gle measurement (Figure 1). Multi-agent systems built on LLMs produce such logs routinely. Relay chains forward observations, blackboards and shared memories rewrite them [47, 49], debate and round-table protocols circulate them before a decision [6, 15], hierarchical teams summarise them upward [29, 40], and retrieval returns near-duplicates of a single document [7]. Whatever compo-nent pools these messages, whether a judge, a planner, a router or the final answerer, has to decide how much the restatements count. Probability theory gives an unambiguous answer. Classical aggregators avoid it because the dependence between sources is known, estimated, or enforced by the protocol. An LLM aggregator receives text instead. Provenance is written in plain view, in source fields, relay names and attribution lines, but nothing in the pipeline acts on it unless the model does. Repeated content is known to sway LLM answers [37, 52, 66]. In a multi-agent system, three questions decide how much such sway matters: how much a restatement counts relative to an independent observation, whether that weight changes decisions, and which part of the system, the model or the protocol, controls it. We answer them with a measure in units of independent readings. A reported probability is converted into the number of indepen-dent readings whose posterior it equals, so one reading stated once counts as one and three independent readings count as three. A restatement then receives a copy weight 𝑤 . An aggregator that counts sources gives copies 𝑤 = 0, and one that takes every state-ment for a new reading gives them 𝑤 = 1. Because the measure is read from the belief, before any threshold is applied, one set of reports gives the implied decision for every cost structure. The measure also separates two quantities. The copy weight belongs to the model, whereas the restatement count , the number of times the log states a reading, is fixed by the protocol before any model is called. Models and analysis. We query GPT-6 Luna, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (Luna, Sol, Gemini and DeepSeek below) through their official APIs at low reasoning effort, the lowest level all four support, without setting sampling parameters. ## 4 Copy Counting in Three Testbeds 4.1 Every model counts a copy as part of a reading 

On controlled logs, two forwarded copies raise the reported ev-idence from one reading to 1.12 (Sol), 1.42 (Gemini), 1.55 (Luna) and 1.84 (DeepSeek), although their provenance labels mark both records as copies (Table 1). The copy weights are 𝑤 3 = 0.06, 0 .21, 0.27 and 0 .42, so no model counts sources and none counts every 

> 4

statement. Three independent readings in the same format give 2.83 to 2.96, and the discrimination 𝐷 3 between copies and sources ranges from 0.53 (DeepSeek) to 0.92 (Sol). For every model the weight is positive at every reliability and in every domain, with a smallest value of 0.04 (Sol at 𝑞 = 0.80). More statements raise the evidence further, but beyond three each copy adds less (Figure 3a). At 𝑚 = 8 the models report 1.30 to 2.53 readings, an average weight of 𝑤 8 = 0.04 to 0 .22 per copy. The conflict probes fix the oracle without any appeal to cali-bration. When one reading stated three times meets one opposing reading (Copied tie, 𝑒 ∗ = 0), the models lean toward the copied value by 0.11 to 0.86 readings, whereas a plain tie between two sensors gives 0.00. When it meets two opposing readings (Copied minority, 𝑒 ∗ = −1), 4% (Sol) to 39% (DeepSeek) of the reports side with the copied reading. Three statements of one sensor then outvote two sensors. 

## 4.2 The weight follows restated values 

The controls confirm the first prediction of Section 2.4. Records that point to the original (Pointers) or pad the log to the length of two copies (Padding) leave ˆ 𝑒 at one reading, and so does padding matched to eight copies. Neither the number of records nor the length of the log adds evidence, and references carry no weight. A single record whose relay trace repeats the value (Packed) gives 1.10 to 1.75 readings, close to the 1.12 to 1.84 of three separate records, so the number of senders matters little. Records that claim to match the original without stating its value (Claims) carry little weight for three models (1.01 to 1.07) and half a reading for DeepSeek (1.54). The form of the log modulates the weight without removing it. Dropping the provenance labels raises ˆ 𝑒 on Copies to 1.20 to 1.97, and writing the same records as prose lowers it to 1.05 to 1.55. Format and labels matter only for copies, since three independent readings give 2.82 to 2.96 readings in every format, with or without labels. The evidence rises with the number of times a value is written, whoever writes it. 

## 4.3 Copy counting is mostly all or nothing 

The mean weight hides how it arises. On Copies, from 47% (DeepSeek) to 91% (Sol) of reports give the one-reading posterior to within 0.15 readings, 4% to 35% give the three-reading posterior of full counting, and the rest fall in between. Fully counting reports carry 71% to 84% of the excess evidence. The copy weight thus measures mainly how often a model counts fully, the mixture case of Section 2.4, and a mean below the commit boundary can hide reports far above it (Section 4.4). The log does not fix which way a reply goes. Asked the same log three times, a model counts the copies in some replies but not in others for 16% to 42% of logs. With eight statements the reports spread out. Where eight readings can be registered (𝑞 ≤ 0.70, since reported probabilities are clipped at 0.999), at most 6% of reports count all eight, and 12% to 69% fall between one and eight.

## 5 Merging Copies Before the Decision 

Section 4 places the inflation where the aggregator meets restated text. Copies can therefore be merged on either side of that meeting: by the aggregator that reads the log, or by the protocol that writes it. 

## 5.1 Merging at the aggregator 

A declaration is a paragraph in the aggregator’s system text that states what a copy contributes, and it cuts most of the copy weight. On controlled logs, a declaration that forwarded records add no evidence brings ˆ 𝑒 on Copies to 1.00 to 1.01 for three models and to 1.15 for DeepSeek ( 𝑤 3 ≤ 0.08, Table 1, lower part), and two further wordings, one about relays and one about copies, have the same effect ( 𝑤 3 ≤ 0.09). Unlike a general warning against repetition [52], the declaration names the records it applies to. A Packed record has an empty forwarded_from field, and on such records the evidence under the same declaration stays at 1.02 to 1.33 readings on the 500-item subset, against 1.09 to 1.78 without a note. The response to independent readings moves by 0.06 readings or less, and the share of reports that side with the copied minority falls to 3% or less. A counting instruction, which asks the model to count each distinct reading once without saying why, is weaker on its own (𝑤 3 = 0.01 to 0 .18) and brings 𝑤 3 to 0.03 or less when added to the declaration. Documentation alone does not merge copies. A field guide explaining that forwarded_from names the record a copy came from leaves the copy weight at 0.04 to 0.37, against 0.08 to 0.40 without it. Placebos show that the declaration works through its content and not through its length or position. A paragraph of the same length about timestamps and the answer format changes the copy weight by at most 0.03. The placebo that points most directly at the forwarded records, a note on how they are displayed, raises ˆ 𝑒 

on Copies by 0.27 to 0.39 readings for Luna, Gemini and DeepSeek and leaves Sol unchanged. Drawing attention to copies without a reason to merge them makes them count more. More reasoning also lowers the weight. High effort brings it to 0.01 (Sol) to 0.20 (DeepSeek), and switching reasoning off raises it to 0.40 for Luna and 0.55 for DeepSeek. The aggregator can also merge copies explicitly. On agent-written logs, extracting the distinct readings first and answering from that list brings one-observer logs to 0.95 to 0.99 readings, with no early commitment in any of the 2,000 one-observer logs per model (Ta-ble 2). The declaration, which there states that agents without sen-sors can only repeat readings, brings them to 1.02 to 1.19. On web documents a copy has to be recognised from its content, and the declaration reaches less far. It brings an exact duplicate to 𝜌 = 0.07 to 0 .18 and a republished copy to 0.09 to 0.30, while summaries, the copies furthest from their original, keep a weight of 0.19 to 0.47. Where copies cannot be recognised in the text, they have to be merged upstream, in the protocol. 

## 5.2 Merging in the protocol 

By Observation 1, once a protocol states each reading only once, the copy weight no longer matters, provided that references carry no weight, as on controlled logs (Section 4.2). The reference rule tests this second prediction. It adds a short paragraph to each agent’s prompt that asks the agent to state each reading in full once and to refer to readings already in the log without restating them. Logs written under the rule are scored with no note, so the rule changes only the protocol. Under the rule, agents without sensors point to a reading instead of repeating it (Figure 4b), and relays pass on pointers such as “see Falcon’s report on PM-21”, which the aggregator resolves against the full log. The share of restating messages falls from 71% to 10%, and the median restatement count falls to one in the relay chain, on the board and in the discussion (Table 3). On one-observer logs from these three protocols, all four aggregators then report 0.95 to 1.03 readings, although their copy weights differ sevenfold. Their mean early commitment, 12% to 14% without the rule, falls to 0.5% or less. DeepSeek, which counts copies most, commits early on 23% to 25% of these logs without the rule and on 1.4% or less with it. The hierarchy confirms the prediction from the other side. Its summaries still restate the reading under the rule (median count 2, 33% of messages), and the evidence stays inflated, by up to 0.22 readings for DeepSeek, in line with a restatement count above one. Pooled over all four protocols, early commitment falls from 11.2% to 1.1%. Under the rule no relay loses a reading, and the discrimination between three observers and one is 0.92 to 0.99 in every protocol. 

## 5.3 Genuine corroboration survives merging 

A fix that made every log look like one reading would be useless. With three observers, where three readings are genuine, merging keeps the reported evidence close to what the same model reports when given the true list of readings (2.90 to 2.96, Table 2). The gap is within 0.06 readings under the reference rule and 0.07 when extracting first, whose list matches the true readings in 93% to 97% of logs, and within 0.02 under the declaration for three models. DeepSeek over-counts genuine corroboration as well, with 3.42 

> 7

readings without merging and 3.09 under the declaration. On web documents the declaration lowers the shift that distinct documents cause by 0.04 to 0.12 log-odds. When the models choose actions, the declaration brings the extra commitment that copies cause to one point or less for three models and to 11 points for DeepSeek, while commitment on three independent readings stays at 89% to 99%. 

Choosing where to merge. The three fixes need different things. A declaration needs copies that the aggregator can recognise in the text, which holds for forwarded records and duplicated documents and holds less for summaries. Extraction costs a second model call per decision, and the answer inherits any reading that the list misses. The reference rule needs control over the agents’ prompts and a log in which every reference resolves, and a protocol that asks agents to summarise keeps some restatement under it. 

## 6 Related Work 

Information flow in LLM multi-agent systems. Debate and round-table protocols can improve reasoning [6, 15], although gains at matched budget are contested [55], majority voting accounts for much of the gain attributed to debate [8], and multi-agent systems fail in recurring ways [5]. Much of this work tunes how agents exchange information, by grouping agents to reduce the cost of de-bate [44], choosing between voting and consensus [33], or shaping the communication topology so that errors propagate less [30, 53]. Agents conform to numerically dominant groups [9, 62], debates among models with similar responses converge on the majority view, shared misconceptions included [17], agents form reputations from second-hand gossip [50], and agreement after an exchange is a poor signal of correctness [10]. Correlated errors shrink the effective size of judge panels [34, 57], and evidence spread across agents often fails to be pooled [41, 67]. In shared memories, deriva-tives of one source form false majorities unless an arbiter estimates how many independent sources there are [43]. Other work tests assumptions that multi-agent designs take for granted, such as the interchangeability of agents that fill the same role [21]. We test an assumption about the component that reads the messages, namely that it counts each source once, and we trace the statements it reads back to the protocol that writes them. 

Repetition, conflict and sources in LLM context. Model answers follow the majority of retrieved evidence and shift with the number of supporting documents [59, 66], and retrieved content that de-parts further from the truth is adopted less often [64]. Sourcing cues are underused [58], paraphrases of one argument flip answers more often than distinct supporting documents [46], and among contra-dictory facts hidden in a long context, models favour the repeated one [37]. Under knowledge conflicts, repeating a less credible source reverses preferences for credible ones whether the repetition is at-tributed to one source or to several. A warning against repetition weakens this effect and fine-tuning reduces it further [52], much as an instruction to ignore irrelevant context reduces distraction [54]. A recent benchmark poses exact Bayesian cases that contrast one report echoed several times with independent reports [1]. Graph agents that reach one origin along redundant paths shift their stated confidence in a direction that depends on the model, and all of them revisit that origin more often [60]. In people, repetition alone raises judged truth [11, 27]. Relative to these studies, we express the effect of repetition in units of observations against an exact posterior, follow it to decisions with explicit costs, and trace it to the agent protocols that create the restatements. 

Aggregation under dependence. Opinion pooling, jury theorems and belief merging make the treatment of dependent sources ex-plicit [14, 18, 23, 35, 38, 63], and social learning models show how repeated exposure distorts beliefs [12, 24, 31]. Persuasion bias, the failure to discount information that reaches an agent repeatedly, gives well-connected sources outsized influence [13], and naive herding treats the actions of others as independent signals although they repeat earlier information [19]. Data-incest correction removes double counting when the network is known [36], and covariance intersection stays consistent when it is not [32]. Pooling reports of one evidential root as if they were independent grows overcon-fident as the reports multiply [3], and leaving the combination to arithmetic with a discount for dependent sources avoids this by design [65]. Provenance semirings separate alternative derivations of a fact from its joint use [25], deduplication removes repeated content [2, 4, 39], and provenance-aware memories for agents are emerging [42, 61]. All of these handle dependence with an explicit model or rule. We measure whether an LLM honours dependence that it is only told about in text. 

Probabilistic reasoning in LLMs. LLMs update approximately like Bayesian agents on in-context evidence [26], and their verbalised probabilities carry calibration error [56]. We measure deviations from the Bayesian posterior in units of independent observations, and the conflict probes of Section 4.1 read over-counting from the direction of the answer. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We measured how much LLM aggregators count restated evidence, in units of independent readings. On controlled logs, web doc-uments and agent-written logs, a copy carries a model-specific fraction of the weight of a source, enough to move decisions that the evidence does not support. Most of this weight comes from occasional full counting, which crosses commit boundaries even where the average belief stays below them. The copy weight acts on a quantity that the protocol sets, and the two can be measured separately: the weight from reported probabilities on paired logs, the restatement count from transcripts. Merging copies before the decision removes most of the inflation, whether the aggregator merges them or the protocol never creates them. For protocol de-sign the results point to a simple practice, in which agents refer to readings already in the log instead of restating them. Our controlled logs make provenance honest and explicit. Labels are correct, copies are exact, and the aggregator sees the full log, so every reference can be resolved. Forged identifiers, relays that alter what they forward and sources with shared failure modes call for an oracle that models these dependencies, a natural next step for this measure. The measure itself needs only reported probabilities on logs that differ in how the evidence is restated, so it applies to any aggregator and any protocol that can be run on such logs.

## A.2 Replies and parsing 

Every prompt asks for a final line of a fixed form. The answer is read from the last line that matches, after deleting * and `. Reasoning text is never parsed. 

• Probability: the pattern below, case-insensitive. A value with % is divided by 100. A value outside [0, 1] is a failure. 

> PROBABILITY\s*[:=]\s*(\d*\.?\d+)\s*(%)?\s*\.?\s*$

• Action: after upper-casing and replacing _ and - by spaces, the pattern below, recorded as Q, P or N.

> ACTION\s*[:=]\s*(QUERY|COMMIT\s+(<POS>|<NEG>))

A reply that does not parse, or that is cut off by the output limit, is requested again up to two more times. A reply that still fails after the third attempt counts as missing.
