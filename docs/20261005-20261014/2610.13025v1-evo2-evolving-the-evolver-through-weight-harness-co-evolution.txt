# Evo 2: Evolving the Evolver through Weight-Harness Co-Evolution 

Chejian Xu *,2 , Shujian Zhang 1, Wenxuan Zhou 1, John Lambert 1, Beck Chen 1, Bo Li 2, Andrew Hard 1 and Rajiv Mathews 1  

> 1Google DeepMind, 2University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign, *Work done as a student researcher at Google DeepMind

Self-evolving agents are increasingly used to improve language model systems without human inter-vention through either weight updates or harness improvements. Existing approaches typically pursue these directions separately: some methods update model weights using self-generated experience, while others rewrite the harness that defines how an agent uses prompts, tools, context, and execution feedback. However, these approaches overlook the relationship between model weights and the agent harness. To connect these processes and ensure effective and smooth agent self-evolving, we propose Evo 2, a framework for weight-harness co-evolution. For harness updates, we design a unified dual-role pipeline that a single policy serves as both a Proposer, which writes harness edits, and a Reflector, which diagnoses their effects and guides revision. For weight updates, harness modifications and their execu-tion outcomes guide the training of both the Proposer and the Reflector. We further develop EvoTIDE, a training pipeline that recovers usable trajectories through edit extraction and prefix continuation, then combines SFT with interleaved GRPO across the two roles. Extensive experiments across three coding benchmarks demonstrate that Evo 2 significantly outperforms existing self-evolution methods. It achieves 82.1% on Polyglot and 48.3% on SWE-bench Verified, compared with 62.6% and 41.0% for the strongest baseline, respectively. We also show that the evolved harnesses transfer to different tasks without further evolution and rank first in cross-benchmark evaluations. Our dual-role harness improvement framework is also able to extend to self-evolution with proprietary models, improving a GPT-5-mini agent’s performance from 24.4% to 48.3%. In addition, Evo 2 generalizes to other tasks smoothly. We extend self-evolution to memory tasks, achieving the best performance on the LoCoMo and LongMemEval benchmarks. These results demonstrate the applicability of the framework Evo 2

across domains and its broad transferability across models and tasks. 

## 1. Introduction 

Self-evolving agents offer a way to improve language model systems through their own experience and without human intervention. Existing approaches typically follow two directions. The first updates 

model weights : a model generates solutions, selects useful outputs using task feedback, and trains on them to improve its ability to solve similar problems (Singh et al., 2024; Zelikman et al., 2022). The second modifies the agent harness : a model inspects and rewrites the program that defines how an agent uses prompts, tools, context, and execution feedback (Robeyns et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). By evaluating these edits and retaining useful changes, an agent improves its task performance through modifications to its own code. These approaches accumulate experience in model parameters and executable programs, respectively, but typically pursue the two forms of improvement separately. Treating these directions separately overlooks the relationship between model weights and the agent harness. The model’s capabilities shape the modifications it proposes and how it interprets execution feedback. In turn, each evaluated modification reveals which changes are useful, why an edit fails, and how different improvements interact. This experience can guide the training of the model responsible for evolution. In particular, proposing edits and diagnosing their effects are skills

We introduce Evo 2, a framework for weight-harness co-evolution in self-evolving agents (Figure 1). We train the model to become a better evolver : to propose useful harness modifications, interpret their outcomes, and revise them accordingly. A better evolver builds better harnesses, whose modifications and execution outcomes provide further experience for training the evolver. Harness evolution, therefore, supplies training signals for weight updates, while the updated model guides subsequent harness search. For harness updates, Evo 2 uses a unified dual-role pipeline in which a single policy serves as both a Proposer and a Reflector . The Proposer writes harness edits, while the Reflector examines their execution outcomes and provides feedback for further revision. Both roles share parameters and learn from the effects of the modifications they produce. We organize the search as a tree of harness variants and introduce crossover searching to combine useful strategies discovered on different branches. The Reflector identifies a strategy that addresses a weakness in the current harness, and the Proposer adapts it to that harness, allowing each branch to build on discoveries made elsewhere in the tree. For weight updates, we develop EvoTIDE (Tree-guided Interleaved Dual-role Evolution) for weight updates, to train the shared policy on experience collected during harness evolution. We collect trajectories from both the Proposer and the Reflector, using edit extraction and prefix continuation to recover usable trajectories from incomplete or partially invalid rollouts. These trajectories support an SFT warm start, followed by interleaved GRPO across the two roles. A maintained parent pool exposes the policy to harnesses at different depths and performance levels, so it learns both to improve strong candidates and to repair weaker ones. The updated policy then generates the proposals and feedback used in subsequent evolution rounds. Extensive experiments across three coding benchmarks demonstrate that Evo 2 outperforms existing self-evolution methods. It achieves 82.1% on Polyglot and 48.3% on SWE-bench Verified, compared with 62.6% and 41.0% for the strongest respective baselines. The evolved harnesses also transfer across benchmarks without further evolution. We further apply the dual-role harness improvement framework to proprietary models with fixed weights and extend self-evolution to memory tasks, achieving the best performance on LoCoMo and LongMemEval. ## 2. Related Work 

Self-improving language models. Self-improvement through training forms an iterative loop: a model generates experience, learns from feedback, and uses its updated weights to collect the next round of training data (Fang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). STaR and ReST EM train on successful model outputs (Singh et al., 2024; Zelikman et al., 2022), while Absolute Zero and R-Zero develop tasks and solvers through self-play (Huang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). Other methods improve training signals through trajectory selection, semantic voting, or learned evaluators (Jiang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2026). EvolveR combines reusable experience with policy reinforcement (Wu et al., 2025). ReVeal, Agent0-VL, and Self-Distillation Zero train generation together with verification or revision (He et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2025); EvoLM and CoNL co-train generation and feedback mechanisms within shared policies (Li et al., 2026; Sui and Hooi, 2026). Evo 2 applies shared-role training to harness modification: EvoTIDE uses supervised fine-tuning and interleaved GRPO to train a unified Proposer–Reflector model on evaluated edits and their outcomes. 

Self-evolving agent harnesses. Agents can also accumulate improvements in the harness that manages their prompts, tools, and control flow. They propose modifications, evaluate the resulting agent, and use the outcomes to guide further changes. SICA edits its own implementation (Robeyns et al., 2025), DGM maintains an archive for branching self-modification (Zhang et al., 2026), and HGM selects promising parents using estimated descendant performance (Wang et al., 2026). Meta-Harness searches over code using prior candidates and execution traces (Lee et al., 2026). GEPA combines reflection with prompt evolution (Agrawal et al., 2026), while GEA shares experience across evolving agents (Weng et al., 2026). The Red Queen Gödel Machine extends evolution to changing evaluation utilities (Iacob et al., 2026). Prior work also demonstrates harness transfer across tasks and models (Lee et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Weng et al., 2026). Evo 2 makes harness search a source of self-improvement for the evolver itself: crossover searching lets the Reflector select a donor strategy and the Proposer adapt it to a recipient harness, while the resulting experience trains both roles to guide subsequent search. We evaluate the framework’s applicability across coding and memory domains as well as the transfer of its evolved harnesses across benchmarks and executors.

## 3. Method 

3.1. Overview 

Evo 2 integrates harness evolution with weight evolution (Figure 2). A harness, ℎ, is the executable program that manages an agent’s prompts, tools, context, and control flow. A language model policy, 

𝜋 𝜃 , serves as the evolver, acting as both Proposer and Reflector through different role prompts. We train its parameters, 𝜃 , on the outcomes of harness modifications. The task executor, denoted 𝑀 exec ,runs inside each candidate harness and remains fixed during co-evolution, so candidate performance measures the effect of changing the harness. Harness evolution proceeds through iterative tree search. Each node stores a harness and its task evaluation records; an edge records the edits made to its parent. At each iteration, we sample a parent, use the Proposer to modify it, and evaluate the resulting candidate. The Reflector then examines the execution feedback and recommends a revision, integration of a strategy from another branch, or retention of the candidate. The Proposer implements any recommended change, which is evaluated in turn. The resulting harnesses extend the tree, while the role trajectories and task outcomes provide data for weight evolution. EvoTIDE uses these data for supervised fine-tuning followed by interleaved reinforcement learning. Updated weights are used in subsequent rounds of search and data collection.

3.3. Weight evolution through EvoTIDE 

EvoTIDE turns the trajectories generated during harness evolution into training data for the shared policy. We first collect an initial dataset and perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We then continue collecting experience from the evolving tree while training both roles with group relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024). 

3.3.1. Data collection 

For each evolution attempt, we record the role prompts, model responses, applied edits, parent and child harnesses, and task evaluation results. One propose-evaluate-reflect-revise cycle provides an autonomous Proposer trajectory, a Reflector trajectory, and, when a revision is requested, a directed Proposer trajectory. The same evaluated harness can also serve as a parent in a later round or contribute a strategy to the crossover library.

Recovering Proposer trajectories. An otherwise useful rollout may fail to produce an executable edit because of how the response is expressed. We use two recovery procedures during data collection. 

Edit extraction handles rollouts that end without an explicit edit action: we search the recorded reasoning and chat messages for concrete file edits and convert them into the required action format. 

Prefix continuation handles a sequence of edits in which a later edit is invalid, for example because of a malformed tool call or an incorrect edit format. We truncate the rollout after the last successfully applied edit, restore the corresponding intermediate harness, and resume generation from that prefix. This preserves the valid earlier edits while allowing the model to regenerate the failed suffix. Recovered proposals pass the same application, startup, and task evaluation checks as other candidates before entering the training data. 

Collecting Reflector trajectories. Evaluated proposals become reflection cases, including edits that improve performance, leave it unchanged, or cause regressions. For each case, we generate feedback and evaluate the candidate obtained by implementing it; a keep response retains the initial candidate. These outcomes provide supervision for the Reflector’s diagnosis and choice of action. Connecting strategies from other branches adds crossover cases, so the dataset includes both local revisions and the integration of previously discovered mechanisms.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Settings Models. Our main experiments use Qwen3.8-27B, served in FP8 with greedy decoding, as the fixed task executor. We use the same model as the evolver: the untrained base model for the baselines and the model fine-tuned by EvoTIDE for Evo 2, where a single policy serves as both Proposer and Reflector. For memory tasks, we additionally use a Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B retriever. In the proprietary-model experiments, GPT-5-mini and Claude Haiku 4.5 each serve as both executor and evolver, with their weights fixed throughout evolution. 

Benchmarks and metrics. We evaluate coding harness evolution on Polyglot, SWE-bench Verified (SWE), and LiveCodeBench (LCB). We report held-out pass@1 on Polyglot tasks over three evaluation runs, the resolved rate on SWE-bench Verified instances, and pass@1 on LCB problems. Proprietary models are evaluated on a 60-task SWE-bench Verified subset. For memory tasks, we evolve harnesses on LoCoMo and report mean F1 on three held-out conversations. We also evaluate the same harnesses on LongMemEval-S using accuracy over 500 instances, with GPT judging and three evaluation runs. 

Baselines. We compare against four representative self-evolving agent frameworks: SICA (Robeyns et al., 2025), which edits its own implementation along a single lineage; DGM (Zhang et al., 2026), which maintains an open-ended archive and branches self-modifications from stored agents; 

HGM (Wang et al., 2026), which selects parents based on the estimated performance of their descen-dants; and GEA (Weng et al., 2026), which enables concurrently evolving agents to share experience. All baselines use the untrained model as the evolver and follow their own selection rules. 

4.2. Self-Evolution on Coding Benchmarks Evo 2 evolves the strongest harness on every benchmark. Table 1 reports the held-out performance of the best harness evolved by each method on each benchmark. On Polyglot, Evo 2 achieves 82.1% pass@1, compared with 62.6% for the strongest baseline, GEA. On SWE-bench Verified, it resolves 48.3% of held-out instances, compared with 41.0% for HGM. On LiveCodeBench, Evo 2 reaches 58.0% pass@1, while the four baselines range from 39.0% to 56.0%. These results demonstrate consistent improvements across all three coding benchmarks.

4.5. Self-Evolution on Memory Tasks 

To evaluate the framework in another domain, we instantiate it with a memory harness and perform self-evolution on LoCoMo. We retain the Proposer–Reflector interaction, crossover search, and training procedure, while adapting the editable harness and task evaluator to memory tasks. Each search evaluates 1,500 questions drawn from seven training conversations. Table 4 reports the results. 

Evo 2 outperforms the strongest baseline on LoCoMo and transfers better to LongMemEval. 

On LoCoMo, Evo 2 achieves 60.4 F1, compared with 56.2 for HGM and 53.2–55.8 for SICA, DGM, and GEA. > 10 Evo 2: Evolving the Evolver through Weight-Harness Co-Evolution

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented Evo 2, a framework for weight-harness co-evolution in self-evolving agents. A single policy serves as both Proposer and Reflector, learning from harness modifications and their execution outcomes to improve its ability to propose edits and guide revision. Crossover searching combines useful strategies across branches, while EvoTIDE trains the shared policy through trajectory recovery, SFT, and interleaved GRPO. Experiments on coding and memory tasks demonstrate improved task performance and transfer of evolved harnesses across benchmarks. The dual-role search also supports harness improvement with fixed proprietary models. Our analyses show that weight evolution improves search reliability and that crossover searching reduces the evaluation budget needed to reach strong performance. Evolving the evolver allows experience to accumulate in both the executable harness and the policy that guides future evolution.
