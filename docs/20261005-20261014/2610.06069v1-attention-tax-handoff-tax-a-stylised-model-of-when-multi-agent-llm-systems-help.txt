# ABSTRACT 

Recent work on multi-agent LLM systems reaches sharply different conclusions: some results show that a single agent with the same information and compute should dominate a delegated system, others that multi-agent gains grow with task depth. We argue that much of the disagreement comes from modelling different bottlenecks, and introduce a stylised reliability model built around two trade-offs. Decomposition reduces the burden of long contexts but incurs a handoff tax when information is compressed or transferred between agents. Redundancy gains from multiple samples, but its benefit depends on how much their failures are shared. With reasoning budget, verification, and task structure added, the model yields two crossover conditions: decomposition becomes preferable once the attention cost avoided by resetting context exceeds the handoff cost, and parallel sampling at equal budget is eventually preferable when its shared-failure floor lies below the error floor of one agent thinking longer. We connect these regimes to recent theoretical and empirical results. On a ledger-reconciliation task we measure the context-degradation curve and the handoff tax from single-agent and handoff runs alone. From these the model places the crossover at depth 10 and predicts decomposition to win at depths 20, 50, and 100. It does, on step-level and final-balance accuracy, and the decomposed system’s success, which the prediction never sees, lands within 9 percentage points of the predicted rate at every depth. 

Keywords multi-agent systems · large language models · context length · test-time compute · reliability 

# 1 Introduction 

There is a live argument about whether multi-agent LLM systems are worth building, and it has now been formalised by both sides. Tran and Kiela [1] argue from the data processing inequality that under equal reasoning-token budgets a single agent is more information-efficient than any system that passes summaries between agents, and they confirm it on multi-hop question answering across three model families. Ao et al. [2] prove the decision-theoretic version: without new exogenous signals, any delegated network is dominated by a centralised Bayes decision maker with access to the same information. On the other side, Tang et al. [3] prove that the relative gain of a multi-agent system over a single agent increases in both task depth and task width, and grows without bound in depth. Meyerson et al. [4] then solve a million-step task with zero errors by decomposing it into single-step subtasks, something no single agent comes close to. And Jwalapuram et al. [5] put both outcomes in one table: a self-consistency baseline outperforms six automated multi-agent frameworks almost everywhere they test, often at under a tenth of the cost, while an expert-designed decomposition raises GPT-5 from 57.0 to 96.5 on a context-heavy task the automated frameworks also fail. Taken together, the papers cannot all describe the same world, and yet each of them is a theorem. Our claim is that much of the contradiction comes from bookkeeping. Each of these models fixes some of five quantities and lets the others move, and a quantity one side fixes is often exactly the one the other side varies. The sceptics’ dominance theorems hold for a single agent whose per-step accuracy does not depend on how much sits in its context, and where they relax

MAKER explicitly analyses voting costs and passes task state between steps. This paper writes down a stylised model with all five quantities and works out what happens in it. A task is a chain of L steps. A step is an LLM call whose failure probability ε(c, b ) depends on the context load c it attends over and the thinking budget b it receives. Two structural primitives are available: decomposition, which gives each agent a fresh context at the price of a handoff loss h, and redundancy, which runs N parallel calls whose failures are dependent because they share a base model. Verifiers at boundaries recover some errors. Budgets are matched per step. Each prior result then corresponds to a restriction of the model. We say for each whether the correspondence is exact, structural, or an analogy. The decomposition question has a crossover. For single-step agents there is a unique depth L∗

past which decomposition wins, and it exists if and only if the curve’s asymptotic rise above a fresh agent’s operating point exceeds one handoff’s cost. For arbitrary chunk sizes the criterion is the total tax a fresh agent avoids over its whole run, which can exceed the handoff cost even when the single-step gap does not. A flat curve gives no crossover at any chunk size, which is the sceptics’ conclusion. In the linear regime the crossover and the chunk size have closed forms. The redundancy question compares two floors under a latent-rate mixture model of the samples: parallel sampling at equal budget wins for all sufficiently large sample counts whenever its shared-failure floor, ρ ε in our common-shock example, is below the single agent’s saturated thinking error ε∞. Equal floors require a finite-budget comparison. Two measurements accompany the model. The first is a padded-context pilot: a thousand calls to a self-hosted gpt-oss-20b at five context loads, which measures the curve and nothing else, and which showed that the curve’s shape depends on what one counts as a step failure. The second is the test the model asks for. On a ledger-reconciliation task with two long-range dependencies, we measured the in-task curve from single-agent runs and the handoff tax from paired continue-versus-handoff runs, and computed from those two measurements alone the depth at which decomposition into five-step agents should start winning and the success rate of each system at every tested depth. The decomposed system won at every depth, as predicted. The predicted crossover sits at depth 10 and the shallowest depth we ran was 20, so the runs test the winner past the crossover and not the crossing itself.

# 2 The three camps, and what each holds fixed 

Sceptics. Tran and Kiela [1] model the messages between agents as a Markov chain on the task information and apply the data processing inequality. Their dominance result assumes “perfect context utilization”: the single agent extracts from a long context everything it would extract from a short one. They then relax it. Their Section 3.1 models an effective context degraded by an exogenous corruption level, shows by the same inequality that the single agent’s achievable error is monotone in that level, and their Section 5.3 corrupts contexts by masking and substitution and finds the ordering reverse at heavy corruption. What they do not have is degradation as a function of the load a trajectory accumulates, a depth or chunk-size model, or a way to compute where the reversal falls from measured quantities. That 2Attention Tax, Handoff Tax is the gap this paper fills, and the sceptic corner of our model is their undegraded case. Ao et al. [2] work at the level of information structure and deliberately not at the level of token generation. Their delegated decision network has nodes with decision rules, a common signal (the shared context), private exogenous signals (tool outputs), and messages along edges. Proposition 6 there says that with no new exogenous signals the network is dominated by a centralised Bayes decision maker, and Theorem 8 says the loss from communication is an expected posterior divergence, equal to the conditional mutual information I(Y ; H | M ) under log loss. The centre in their model has no capacity constraint, and a capacity constraint is the only thing our model adds to theirs. The empirical audits lean the same way. Xu et al. [6] show a single agent with multi-turn interaction matches homogeneous workflows, Orogat et al. [7] find that orchestration depth limits scalability independently of the model, Kim et al. [8] fit a predictive model of when adding agents helps (cross-validated R2 = 0 .37 ) and find gains on some task families and losses of up to 70 per cent on sequential planning, and Jwalapuram et al. [5] report that many automated multi-agent frameworks collapse into self-consistency in practice. Cemri et al. [9] supply the failure taxonomy behind much of this. 

Optimists. Tang et al. [3] define per-step success σ(w) = uw for a task of width w (we rename their symbols to keep ours free) and put Ssingle (d, w ) = σ(w)d against a multi-agent success in which N agents answer each step independently and an aggregator picks the correct answer with probability r whenever some agent has it. Their printed Definition 2.3 places r outside the d-th power, while their Propositions 2.1 and 2.2 and proofs use the per-step form 

[ r(1 − (1 − σ(w)) N ) ] d, which we take to be the intended one. Their relative gain increases in d and w, saturates at 

(rN )d − 1 in width, and is unbounded in depth. Two features matter for what follows. The single agent’s per-step success is the same σ(w) as each worker’s, so their single agent does not degrade with context either. And nothing is budget-normalised: the multi-agent system spends N calls per step at every one of d steps. Meyerson et al. [4] supply the engineering version of the same idea. They decompose a Towers-of-Hanoi run into a million single-step subtasks with a first-to-ahead-by-k vote at each, cite context growth as the reason a single agent fails, and report a per-step error rate that stays flat as the task scales once each agent sees only what its step needs. Su and Wu [10] study the training-time question and get a PAC dichotomy: independent subtasks make multi-agent fine-tuning more sample-efficient, dependent subtasks add a quadratic penalty from error propagation. 

Redundancy and aggregation. A third literature never decomposes anything and only asks what N parallel answers buy. Yang et al. [11] bound the information any transcript can carry by H(Y |X) and introduce an effective channel count K with a complementarity rate α, under which residual uncertainty contracts like (1 − α)K . Homogeneous agents stop adding channels, so scaling saturates, and heterogeneous ensembles keep going. Patel et al. [12] give the exponential reliability gain of consensus voting under independence. Yang et al. [13] show in an appendix how that gain fails under correlated correctness. Maryanskyy et al. [14] derive a crossover in selector quality below which team diversity hurts and above which it helps, which in our notation is a threshold on the selector’s recall r. Choi et al. [15] prove that, under their conditions, debate conserves an agent’s expected belief, so debate cannot systematically raise expected belief above what the initial round already carries, and Liu et al. [16] add that correlated errors make debate converge on shared mistakes. None of this is new to statistics. Ladha [17] worked out the Condorcet jury theorem under correlated votes, and Kuncheva and Whitaker [18] catalogued how ensemble diversity relates to ensemble accuracy. Snell et al. [19] supply the empirical cousin on the single-model side: parallel and sequential test-time compute trade off differently depending on the problem. 

Verification and context management. Aghazadeh and Pishro-Nik [20] give the most complete reliability theory we know of. A task is a vector of coupled binary subclaims, an architecture is a sparse role-typed factor graph whose check nodes are noisy Boolean verifiers, and three failure modes (an agent abstains, a verifier returns nothing, a message is lost) propagate as set-valued messages under iterative decoding. They prove a density-evolution theorem in the style of Richardson and Urbanke [21], characterise finite-length failures by certificate-stopping sets, and solve cost-constrained architecture optimisation. The theory is erasure-only by design, and their limitations section lists what is outside it: confidently wrong messages, correlated agent failures from shared training data, adaptive routing, and “compounding prompt-context failures specific to LLM-style verifiers.” On the context side, Sun et al. [22] fold completed sub-trajectories out of the active context and scale a single agent to long horizons that way, which is decomposition without a second agent, and Kang et al. [23] and Xia et al. [24] treat compaction as the lever. Those methods pay our handoff tax. The closest precursor to the decomposition side of our model is Xu et al. [25], who split the error of divide-and-conquer processing of a long input into task noise (cross-chunk dependence), model noise (confusion that grows with context size), and aggregator noise, and show by varying chunk size that chunking pays when model noise grows fast enough and task noise is manageable. Their two leading terms are the parallel-chunking analogues of our handoff tax and curve. Liu et al. [26] put finite context, a message-fidelity function, and shared-failure correlation into one budgeted theory and derive phase transitions for star, chain, and tree organisations, with chains degrading unless new evidence enters. Their context limit is a hard window on fan-in and their validation is synthetic. The curve. Whether per-step accuracy depends on context load has a broad but not uniform empirical answer. Liu et al. [28] found a U-shaped dependence on where relevant information sits. Hsieh et al. [29] found most long-context models fail well below their advertised length. Modarressi et al. Scaffold effects. Zhang et al. [35] and Yao et al. [36] show that scaffold choice moves agent benchmark scores as much as model choice. Any placement of a published benchmark in our regime map is therefore qualitative, and we do not attempt a quantitative one. 

# 3 Model 

The model is stylised. It is built to make the five quantities the literature holds fixed into explicit parameters, and the price of that is a first-order treatment of everything else. We state each simplification where it is made. Table 2 collects the symbols.

3.4 Redundancy and verification 

At any step the system may instead run N parallel calls and aggregate. Parallel calls to the same base model do not fail independently. We assume the N correctness indicators are generated by an exchangeable Bernoulli mixture: conditionally i.i.d. given a latent per-item failure rate p drawn from some law μ (Section 5). For infinite exchangeable sequences this is de Finetti’s representation, and for the finite samples we actually draw it is an assumption. The worked example throughout is the common-shock law: with probability ρ a single latent draw Z ∼ Bernoulli(1 − ε) is copied to all N outputs, and otherwise each output is an independent Bernoulli(1 − ε). A one-line computation gives 

Cov( Xi, X j ) = ρ ε (1 − ε), so the pairwise correlation is exactly ρ. Aggregation is either a majority vote on correctness or, following Tang et al. [3], a selector that returns the correct answer with probability r whenever some sample has it. A verifier at a boundary checks the fresh agent’s step before it is passed on, with recall q on true failures, and never turns a correct output into an incorrect one. Generator and verifier can share the failure’s cause, and we let ρgv be the probability that they do, so effective recall is qeff = q(1 − ρgv ). # 7 Reductions 

Table 3 states, for each prior result, the restriction of the model under which its conclusion is recovered and how tight the correspondence is. Three are discussed below. 

The sceptic corner. Under a flat curve, Corollary 1 says the single agent weakly dominates at every depth and chunk size, which is the conclusion of Tran and Kiela [1] under their premise and of Ao et al. [2] for a centre with no capacity limit. The first is structural: same premise, same conclusion, a bookkeeping argument in place of an information inequality. The second is analogical, because their loss is a Bayes-risk gap under a proper scoring rule and ours is an operational log-reliability, and we do not identify the two. 

The optimist corner is a redundancy result. The result of Tang et al. [3] is often cited as a decomposition result. Their single agent has the same per-step success σ(w) as each of their workers, so there is no attention tax in their model, and their workers never hand anything off, so there is no handoff tax either. What their multi-agent system has is N independent answers per step and a selector with recall r. In our terms that is the redundancy primitive at every step with ρ = 0 , the any-correct aggregator, and no budget constraint. Their unbounded growth in depth is the compounding of a per-step advantage: whenever r(1 − (1 − σ)N ) > σ the ratio of the two success probabilities is 

 r(1 − (1 − σ)N )/σ d, which grows geometrically in d. Their width effect lowers σ = uw and so raises the value of redundancy per step. Both survive in our model as long as ρ is small and compute is free. Impose the budget and Theorem 6 takes over. Impose shared failure and the per-step floor becomes (1 − r) + rρε instead of 1 − r. Tang et al. [3] prove a correct theorem about free, independent redundancy, and it says little about the decomposition question it is often cited for. 

The PAC dichotomy is an analogy across phases. Su and Wu [10] find that multi-agent fine-tuning is more sample-efficient when subtasks are independent (Theorem 4.3 there) and worse when they are dependent (Theorem 4.2, a K2

penalty from error propagation). In our inference-time model a dependency edge is an edge where the downstream step needs information from upstream, which is where h > 0, and an independent edge is where h = 0 . We record it as a correspondence across a change of phase and claim nothing more.

Parallel sampling wins at equal budget below the threshold and loses above it, and the empirical success of self-consistency [38] says the shared-failure mass for a well-prompted sampled chain is often below threshold on the tasks people try. Claim A dec (1, 2): under equal information and equal budget, a single agent that uses its context fully weakly dominates any delegated network. For any curve that rises above the fresh agent’s operating point by more, in total, than a handoff costs, decomposition wins at every sufficiently large depth and not below its first crossover. Failure definitions. The curve is a property of the model, the task, and the failure definition together. The same thousand calls give a flat curve and a steep one, and a naive last-integer grader produced a third by treating a remainder in responses without an answer line as the final answer. The mechanism in the steep case is not reasoning collapse but decay of compliance with an instruction that sits far from the point of use, which is the phenomenon Liu et al. [28] measured for facts, seen here for an instruction. Two consequences follow if the mechanism is what it appears to be. A practitioner who repeats the format instruction next to the question should flatten the strict curve, and the model predicts this because it shortens the distance the instruction must survive. And a paper that reports a single-agent win on short-context benchmarks and a multi-agent win on long-context ones may be measuring exactly this: not what the model can compute, but what it still remembers it was asked to do. 

# 10 The experiment 

The model suggests one direct test. Measure the curve and the handoff tax, compute the depth at which decomposition should start to win and how often each system should succeed, then compare with both systems run on the same ledgers. We ran that test on the ledger task of the running example. 

Task. A ledger of L transactions with an exact integer running balance, starting at 50,000. Six transaction kinds: deposit, withdrawal, a percentage fee on the current balance rounded down, a hold that removes an amount now, a release that returns a hold’s amount three to twelve steps later but names the hold only by id, and a reversal that undoes a deposit or withdrawal three to twelve steps back, also named only by id. The last two are the long-range dependencies: the amount has to come from earlier context or from a handoff note. Each transaction block is padded with digit-free administrative memo lines to about 400 tokens, and the measured context growth was 398 tokens per step. Ledgers are generated from a seed, and the same 100 ledgers are used at each depth by both systems, so every comparison is paired. A step is graded on its increment, whether the reported balance moved by the true amount for that transaction, so a step is not penalised for a balance error it inherited. A ledger is graded twice: on whether every non-fee increment is right and every handoff note carries its balance forward unchanged (the stationary grading, explained below) and on whether its final balance is exactly right. 

Systems and protocol. The single agent runs one growing conversation, one call per transaction, so its context at step t is c0 + kt with c0 = 197 tokens. The decomposed system gives each agent m = 5 consecutive transactions in a fresh conversation. At each boundary the outgoing agent is asked for a handoff note of about 300 tokens (225 words) carrying the balance, every open hold, and the id and amount of the last twelve transactions, and the incoming agent starts from that note. The handoff tax was measured on a separate pool of 100 ledgers by the paired design of Definition 1: twelve transactions run once, then the next five run twice, continuing in the same conversation and starting from a note, at note allowances of 100, 300, and 900 tokens (produced notes 137, 183, and 255 tokens). The model is gpt-oss-20b on a hosted OpenAI-compatible endpoint (provider recorded in the run configuration), at reasoning level low, temperature 0.7, and a 512-token allowance per step call including reasoning, which is b, the same for every step call in both systems. Handoff notes were separate calls with a 600-token allowance, so the writing agent’s own step budget was not reduced: the experiment sits at ∆o = 0 in the accounting of Section 3, and the note tokens are overhead on top of matched step budgets. Actual generation per ledger, reasoning plus answer, averaged 806, 2,873, and 10,046 tokens for the single agent at depths 20, 50, and 100, against 1,611, 4,797, and 10,195 for the decomposed system including its notes (770, 2,001, and 4,078 for its step calls alone). # 11 Scope and extensions 

The model is first order in three places. Product form ignores dependence along a trajectory. When failures cluster on hard instances it overstates the single agent’s loss, which is the direction seen on the ledger, and a dependent-failure version of the crossover is a natural extension. The budget is fixed per step rather than in total, and the ledger experiment matches systems the same way. The handoff tax is one scalar per boundary, related to the information-theoretic loss of Ao et al. [2] by analogy. The redundancy floor depends on the whole mixing law, of which the common-shock law is one example. The measurements cover one model on two tasks. The quantities the model asks for next are the boundary verifier recall of Section 6, the thinking floor of Section 5, and curves for further models, each of which turns into a direct test of a result above. 

# 12 Conclusion 

Inside the model, the single-versus-multi-agent question turns on one curve and one tax rather than on the number of agents. Decomposition trades information for attention: it pays a handoff tax at every boundary to buy back per-step accuracy, and it is worth it when the tax a fresh agent avoids over its run exceeds what the handoff costs, net of what fresh-context verifiers catch. Redundancy trades budget for independence: it eventually wins at equal compute whenever the mass the samples place on shared failure lies below the thinking floor of one agent. The formal results we could find in the 2025 to 2026 literature correspond to these two statements with some quantities pinned, exactly in a few cases and by analogy in most, and the empirical record, including the one audit that reports both outcomes side by side, sorts along the same lines. The failure definition is part of the curve: the pilot curve was flat for the task and steep for the instruction. On the ledger task, measuring the context curve and handoff tax from single-agent and handoff runs alone was enough to predict the winning architecture and the decomposed system’s success to within 9 percentage points, with the crossover itself below the tested range. This turns the framework from a retrospective explanation of conflicting results into a measurable architecture-selection rule in the regime tested here.
