As large language models become more powerful, self-evolving agents are able to tackle challenging tasks including AI for machine learning (AI4ML). In AI4ML, while empirical verification is available, it often requires computationally costly model training and evaluation, limiting the speed and scale of agent evolution. Yet verification efficiency remains underexplored, and frontier models provide only limited gains when used directly as idea selectors. We address this gap with specialized idea-level critic models that predict whether a proposed ML modification will improve upon the current solution, allowing agents to screen ideas and concentrate verification resources on the most promising candidates. We train the critic models through supervised fine-tuning on high-quality critiques synthesized by Gemini-3.1-Pro, followed by GRPO to further improve their predictive accuracy. Empirically, our critic models outperform Gemini-3.1-Pro in static idea evaluation, and these gains extend to agent inference, continual learning, and policy training. During inference-time evolution, they improve final solution quality under the same verification budget by selecting more promising ideas, with further gains from continual learning. During policy training, they serve as learned reward models, reserving empirical verification for uncertain cases and enabling substantially more policy updates with the same verification resources. Together, these results show that idea-level critic models help ML agents discover better solutions and learn stronger proposal policies under limited verification budgets.

# 1 Introduction 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have spurred growing interest in self-improving and evolutionary agents, which iteratively propose modifications and retain successful variants based on empirical feedback (Novikov et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Sharma, 2025). These efforts demonstrate unprecedented success on challenging tasks such as AI for machine learning (AI4ML), where agents develop and refine ML solutions through implementation and empirical evaluation (Huang et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024). However, AI4ML presents a distinctive verification challenge: while available, empirical verifiers often require computationally costly model training and evaluation. This execution cost accumulates across candidates and evolutionary rounds, directly limiting how many alternatives an agent can explore under a fixed compute budget. Addressing this issue by simply using a frontier model to screen candidates offers limited relief: in our OpenEvolve experiments, directly prompting Gemini-3.1-Pro as an idea selector yields only modest gains (Section 4). These limitations motivate us to ask: can we train specialized models to assess proposed ML modifications before empirical verification, reserving expensive execution for those most likely to improve the current solution? In this work, we develop a training pipeline for specialized idea-level critic models that screens proposed ML improvements before verification, reducing reliance on computationally costly empirical verification. Given a task description, a parent solution, and a proposed child modification, the critic model predicts whether the modification will improve upon the parent. To train the critic model, we collect realistic idea-evolution trajectories from live ML agent runs and label parent–child pairs using their empirical execution outcomes. We then use Gemini-3.1-Pro (Google Gemini Team, 2026) to synthesize critiques, retain critiques consistent with the execution-derived labels through rejection sampling, and train the critic model through supervised 1arXiv:2610.08993v1 [cs.AI] 6 Oct 2026 Figure 1 We train an idea-level critic model from ML agent evolution trajectories through SFT on soft-rubric critiques followed by GRPO. The critiques follow a soft rubric-based reasoning format: the critic model compares the parent and child under every task-specific criterion while retaining flexibility in weighting the criteria and combining their judgments. Our ablations show that this structure supports continued improvement during further RL alignment, whereas unconstrained reasoning leads to performance degradation. We hold out a portion of the collected data to form ML-Idea-Critic-Bench , a static benchmark for measuring training gains and comparing the idea-level assessment capabilities of different models. On this benchmark, our Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B critic models achieve three-way directional accuracies of 60.7% and 63.3%, respectively, substantially outperforming Gemini-3.1-Pro at 46.6%. We evaluate how these predictive gains translate into improvements in agent inference, continual learning, and policy training. First, integrated into OpenEvolve (Sharma, 2025) with Gemini-3.1-Pro as the idea proposer, the critic models screen candidate ideas before verification, improving final solution quality on MLS-Lite-Bench under matched verification budgets. Second, continual learning allows the critic models to adapt using empirical feedback accumulated during the current evolution run, further improving the final solutions. Third, while critic model improves evolution by prioritizing the most promising candidates, the quality of ideas generated remain constraint by the fixed proposer. We therefore extend the critic model from selecting among existing ideas to improving the idea-proposing policy itself. Here, the critic model serves as a learned reward model for GRPO training of the idea-proposing policy, assigning rewards to confident predictions and routing uncertain cases to empirical verification. This reduces the real-verification cost during training, enabling 2.75 × as many policy updates under the same verification budget. In summary, our main contributions are below: • We establish an effective training pipeline for idea-level critic models from real ML agent evolving trajectories. Through fine-tuning from soft-based reasoning critiques, our critic model outperforms both their base models and Gemini-3.1-Pro in static idea evaluation. • We show that directly prompting an off-the-shelf frontier model, Gemini-3.1-Pro, as an idea selector yields only limited gains in OpenEvolve, whereas our trained critic models improve inference-time evolution under fixed verification budgets by concentrating empirical verification on the most promising proposals. • We extend the use of critic models from idea selection to policy training by using their predictions as rewards and reserving empirical verification for uncertain cases. To make idea-level evaluation task-aware, we structure the critic model’s reasoning around a set of task-specific criteria. The critic model must compare the parent and child solutions under each criterion before reaching a final verdict. For each ML task, we use Claude-4.8-Opus (Anthropic, 2026) in a multi-round probing process to analyze the task setup, data, evaluation metric, and modeling constraints. This process produces a detailed task understanding for task t and a fixed set of task-specific criteria, which are generated once and reused across all examples from the same task. Accordingly, each critic model input contains the task description, parent solution, proposed child solution, task understanding, and task-specific criteria, with example shown in Appendix C.2. 

Soft-rubric reasoning. The critic model follows a soft-rubric reasoning template. It first analyzes every criterion and compares the parent and child solutions under that criterion, then freely determines the relative importance of the criteria and aggregates its analyses into a final verdict. We call this design a soft rubric 

because the critic model must evaluate the fixed criteria but remains unrestricted in its analysis and weighting strategy. Appendix D and Appendix E provide the complete prompt instructions and a reasoning example, respectively. 

2.3 Training Data and ML-Idea-Critic-Bench Construction 

Dataset overview. The dataset contains approximately 28K execution-labeled examples: approximately 12.5K in the SFT/evaluation pool and 15.5K in the GRPO training pool. We divide the former into approximately 11K SFT training examples and 1.5K static evaluation examples, which constitute ML-Idea-Critic-Bench .The evaluation set contains roughly 500 examples from each of the three teacher-assistance strata defined below. 

ML idea-evolution trajectories. We collect parent–child pairs from MLE-Dojo (Qiang et al., 2025) and MLS-Bench (Lyu et al., 2026), with Gemini-3.1-Pro generating both candidate ideas and their implementations. Initial parents are drawn from the baseline implementations provided by MLS-Bench or constructed as basic executable solutions for MLE-Dojo. Each trajectory example is represented as xi = ( ti, p i, a i, y i), comprising a task description ti, a parent idea pi, a child idea ai, and an outcome label yi derived from execution. An execution first yields one of three outcomes: Improve , Tie , or Lose . To provide more precise targets for critique synthesis in the SFT/evaluation pool, we use the empirical distribution of child performance relative to the parent to further divide Improve into BigWin , MediumWin , and SmallWin , yielding the five-way label space 

yi ∈ BigWin , MediumWin , SmallWin , Tie , Lose ,

which captures both the direction and magnitude of the child’s performance change relative to the parent. The GRPO pool retains the three-way outcome labels and is balanced across these classes by random downsampling. Detailed collection and label-construction are provided in Appendix B. 

Teacher critique synthesis and difficulty strata. To collect high-quality SFT data, we use Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate soft-rubric reasoning critiques whose final predictions follow the same five-way label space. We first sample 8 critiques independently without revealing the execution-derived label yi. An example is assigned to the Sampled stratum if at least one of these critiques predicts yi correctly, in which case we retain one matching critique. If all 8 predictions are incorrect, we identify the most frequently predicted incorrect label, provide a hint explicitly ruling out that option, and sample another 8 critiques. An example is assigned to the 

Revised stratum if at least one of these new critiques predicts yi correctly. If none succeeds, we reveal yi and require Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate reasoning consistent with the correct outcome; these examples form the 

Guided stratum. The resulting SFT/evaluation pool contains three levels of increasing teacher assistance and example difficulty: Sampled (easy, 44.4% ), Revised (hard, 31.6% ), and Guided (harder, 23.9% ).

# 3 ML-Idea-Critic Bench Result 

Metrics. We report four complementary metrics. Improve Accuracy evaluates the binary decision of whether the child improves over its parent. Dir-3 measures three-way accuracy over Improve , Tie , and Lose . F1 reports the F1 score for the binary improvement decision. Finally, 5-Ladder evaluates the exact match accuracy over [ BigWin > MediumWin > SmallWin > Tie > Lose ]. We compare our critics against Gemini-3.1-Pro, the base Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B models, and DeepSeek-GRM-16B/27B (Liu et al., 2025). DeepSeek-GRM is a generalist generative reward model trained with Self-Principled Critique Tuning to generate evaluation principles and critiques before assigning scores. We use the released DeepSeek-GRM-16B 1 and DeepSeek-GRM-27B 2 checkpoints, based on DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Gemma-2-27B, respectively. As shown in Table 1, SFT improves the Dir-3 accuracy of the 9B and 35B critics to 55 .6% and 60 .3% , surpassing Gemini-3.1-Pro ( 46 .6% ). GRPO further improves their accuracy to 

60 .7% and 63 .3% , corresponding to gains of 5.1% and 3.0% over their SFT checkpoints and 14 .1% and 16 .7% 

over Gemini. 

Ablation: Freestyle vs. Soft-Rubric Reasoning. The two reasoning formats perform similarly after SFT, with Dir-3 accuracies of 54 .6% for freestyle reasoning and 55 .6% for soft-rubric reasoning. Under GRPO, however, the freestyle critic deteriorates to 40 .3% , whereas the soft-rubric critic improves to 60 .7% . Qualitatively, the freestyle critic develops shorter analyses and superficial shortcuts, while the required criterion-by-criterion comparisons stabilize reinforcement learning. Results with full metrics can be found in Appendix G.

Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning. Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) is introduced by Shenfeld et al. (2026) for continual learning from demonstrations. SDFT uses the same underlying model as both student and teacher, but provides the teacher with privileged context and distills its token-level predictions into the student along trajectories generated by the student itself. We adapt this idea to test-time alignment by treating the raw scores of parents and verified children as privileged teacher information. At every prefix of the student-generated critique, the teacher produces a next-token distribution, which is the fixed target for updating the student. We minimize the resulting token-level reverse-KL loss over the critique trajectory. We perform one SDFT update after every OpenEvolve round using the three newly verified parent–child pairs, one from each island. 

Test-time GRPO. TTT-Discover (Yuksekgonul et al., 2026) formulated scientific discovery as test-time RL, in which a policy model updates its parameters using experience collected while solving the problem. Following this paradigm, we apply GRPO to the critic model during OpenEvolve. We perform one update every 2 evolution rounds, collecting 6 newly verified parent–child pairs across the three islands. For each pair, we sample 16 critic model rollouts and assign each rollout a reward of +1 if its predicted three-way outcome matches the empirical label and -1 otherwise. As shown in Table 3, SDFT improves downstream evolution more consistently than test-time GRPO. One possible explanation is the limited information in binary correctness rewards combined with the changing outcome distribution during evolution. When all 16 GRPO rollouts receive the same reward, their group-relative advantages vanish, providing no learning signal from the correctness reward. As the parent solution improves, successful modifications can also become rarer, encouraging the critic model to rely on the increasing prevalence of Tie/Lose rather than distinguish promising candidates. Such behavior may achieve high prediction accuracy while providing little value for candidate selection. SDFT instead supplies outcome-conditioned token-level targets along the student’s reasoning trajectory, offering a richer adaptation signal than verdict correctness alone. Our results align with existing work (Shenfeld et al., 2026) that this form of supervision can better support candidate selection and improve final solution quality under a limited verification budget.

5.1 GRPO with Critic-in-Loop 

We further investigate whether the trained critic model can serve as a learned reward model for aligning the idea-proposing policy. We initialize the policy from the base Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B model and optimize it using GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) across the same 95 MLE-Dojo and 46 MLS-Bench training environments used for critic GRPO training. Each training step collects 16 parents, each with 16 rollout ideas, yielding 256 candidate ideas per step. The reward is binary: a solution receives +1 for an Improve outcome and −1 otherwise. We consider two approaches, (1) baseline approach verify-all , where every candidate is empirically evaluated; and (2) critic-in-loop approach, where trained critic models serve as a reward model and replace real verification signals occasionally, depending on the critic’s uncertainty on the candidate idea. Specifically, we use Critic-9B checkpoint serving base Qwen-3.5-9B and Critic-35B checkpoint serving base Qwen-3.5-35B respectfully. 5.2 Policy Model GRPO Result 

For a controlled comparison, each method receives a total budget of 10,000 ground-truth verifications across all training environments. With 256 candidates per training step, verify-all exhausts this budget in approximately 40 steps. With critic-guided reward assignment, only 36 .3% of policy-generated ideas require empirical verification, allowing approximately 110 steps under the same budget. This corresponds to a 2.75 ×

improvement in verification efficiency, measured as the number of candidate ideas assigned training rewards per ground-truth verification. This ratio does not measure wall-clock speedup or total compute savings, since critic inference incurs additional computation cost. We evaluate the resulting policies directly on the MLS-Lite-Bench tasks. As shown in Figure 3, critic-in-loop GRPO improves the average normalized domain score from 19.57 to 25.21 for the 9B model and from 19.29 to 24.55 for the 35B model compared with verify-all GRPO. These results suggest that using critic-model predictions for confident cases allows the saved verification budget to support additional policy updates and thereby shifts the policy toward more promising ML modifications. Full numerical results are reported in Table 15 of Appendix I.1.

# 6 Related Work 

Self-Evolving Agents and ML Agents. ML and self-evolving agents iteratively propose, implement, and evaluate solutions using empirical feedback (Huang et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2025; Novikov et al., 2025; Sharma, 2025). Existing approaches improve this process through search and harness design with fixed pretrained models (Toledo et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026b; Yamada et al., 2025), or by learning proposal policies from feedback (Zhang et al., 2026c; Yan et al., 2026a). Our work complements these directions by learning to evaluate proposed modifications before costly implementation and execution. 

AI for Machine Learning Research. AI-assisted research systems coordinate literature review, experimentation, and manuscript preparation (Schmidgall et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025), while related work studies automated paper reviewing (Tang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a) and research-idea evaluation (Qiao et al., 2026a; Moussa et al., 2025). Closer to our setting, pretrained language model-based evaluation modules assess ML candidates and prioritize promising ones for execution (Foster et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026). We instead make the evaluator itself the primary learned component, training an idea-level critic model to predict the empirical value of proposed ML modifications. 

Critic Models and Generative Reward Models. Conventional reward models typically assign scalar scores to model outputs (Ouyang et al., 2022; Lambert et al., 2025). Critic models and generative reward models produce textual assessments that make the basis of their judgments more explicit (Saunders et al., 2022; McAleese et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b). These assessments support direct output evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Ke et al., 2024), actionable feedback for iterative refinement (Wang et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025), and ranking among sampled candidates (Liu et al., 2025; Zhang 9et al., 2025; Saha et al., 2025). Rubric-guided approaches further structure evaluations across multiple criteria (Viswanathan et al., 2025; Gunjal et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026; Qiu et al., 2026). Related work evaluates intermediate reasoning steps (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024) or uses learned evaluations as reward signals for policy optimization and co-evolution (Shan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b). We focus on proposed modifications to existing ML solutions, whose ground-truth value requires empirical execution. Our critic model learns from empirical parent–child outcomes, supporting both inference-time candidate selection and policy training. More discussion on related work is in Appendix A. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced idea-level critic models as an efficient feedback mechanism for self-improving ML agents together with a training pipeline combining soft-rubric reasoning, SFT, and GRPO. Our critic model not only achieve strong performance in static idea evaluation, but also improve agents across inference-time idea selection, task-specific continual learning, and critic-guided policy optimization. By identifying promising ideas, the critic model enables agents to use limited verification budgets more effectively. As a learned reward model, the critic model also performs 2.75 × more policy-optimization steps under the same budget. Overall, our results establish idea-level critic model as a general and practical interface between idea generation and expensive execution, enabling more efficient ML agent learning and evolution. 10 References

# A Appendix A More Related Work 

Self-Evolving Agents and ML Agents. ML agents automate ML development by translating task specifications into executable solutions, running training and evaluation pipelines, interpreting empirical results, and iteratively revising their approaches (Huang et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2025; Qiang et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Qiao et al., 2026b; Yu et al., 2025, 2026a). Closely related self-evolving and recursively self-improving agents repeatedly modify solutions using feedback from previous iterations (Lu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026a; Novikov et al., 2025; Sharma, 2025). These systems commonly follow a propose–implement–execute–refine loop, and recent work increasingly targets the generation and selection of promising candidates before execution. Some approaches improve the surrounding agent harness while keeping the pretrained model fixed, using structured search and refinement to explore stronger solutions (Toledo et al., 2025; Nam et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2026b; Yamada et al., 2025); others learn proposal policies directly from feedback (Zhang et al., 2026c; Yan et al., 2026a). 

AI for Machine Learning Research. LLM-based agents increasingly automate machine learning research, from literature review and ideation to experimentation, analysis, and manuscript preparation (Lu et al., 2024; Schmidgall et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). Related systems connect idea generation to execution, introduce falsification, and support collaborative or sustained research (Li et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Schmidgall and Moor, 2025; Weng et al., 2026). Recent work further explores research automation at scale and iterative improvement through human feedback and accumulated experience (Tang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a). A complementary line develops AI systems for paper reviewing through multi-agent feedback, aspect-conditioned generation, structured questioning, and rubric-guided evaluation (D’Arcy et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024, 2025; Chang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b). Learned reviewing models also support iterative refinement of research outputs (Weng et al., 2025), while empirical studies examine the effects of LLM use in peer review (Kim et al., 2026). Closer to our setting, pretrained language model-based evaluation modules assess unverified ideas or candidate ML solutions to prioritize empirical execution (Weng et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Foster et al., 2026). Our work focuses on training an execution-grounded idea-level critic model to predict the empirical value of proposed ML modifications. The critic model evaluates each modification relative to an existing solution and learns from outcome labels derived from empirical parent–child comparisons. We develop a training recipe for this prediction task and apply the resulting critic model to candidate selection during inference-time evolution and reward provision during policy optimization. 

Critic Models and General Reward Models. Conventional reward models compress the quality of a model output into a scalar score, providing limited insight and often struggling with open-ended problems whose success depends on multiple interacting factors (Ouyang et al., 2022; Lambert et al., 2025). Critic models and general reward models instead generate reasoning-style analyses before reaching a final judgment (Yao et al., 2022), making their evaluations more interpretable and allowing them to produce more reliable, task-general feedback (Liu et al., 2025; Saunders et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; McAleese et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b). Existing work uses such generative evaluators in several related ways. At inference time, they can directly assess completed outputs (Zheng et al., 2023; Ke et al., 2024), provide actionable feedback for iterative refinement (Saunders et al., 2022; McAleese et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2026), or rank multiple sampled candidates so that only the most promising response is returned (Liu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Saha et al., 2025). In structured scenarios, rubric-guided critiques further regularize multi-dimensional evaluations and mitigate evaluation degradation (Liu et al., 2025; Viswanathan et al., 2025; Gunjal et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Guan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Qiu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026a; Yu et al., 2026b). Other approaches formulate generative critics more explicitly as value models: they estimate the quality or expected return of intermediate states and trajectories (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024), and their judgments can be converted into learning signals for policy optimization, including PPO-style training and adversarial co-evolution (Shan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; 15 Li et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b). These methods have primarily been developed to evaluate language or reasoning outputs produced by policy models. A separate line of work evaluates research idea (Qiao et al., 2026a; Moussa et al., 2025; Falck et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026b) by constructing methodology on human level ideas, which are more tailored to AutoResearch and human-AI collaboration research, which is beyond the scope of our paper. 

# B Data Collection and Label Construction 

B.1 Task Environments and Initial Parent Solutions 

We construct our data from selected task environments in MLE-Dojo (Qiang et al., 2025) and MLS-Bench (Lyu et al., 2026). MLS-Bench provides source code and baseline implementations for each task, which we directly use as the initial parent solutions. MLE-Dojo provides executable task environments but does not include comparable starter solutions. For each selected MLE-Dojo task, we therefore prompt Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate a basic runnable solution, execute it in the corresponding environment, and use its resulting score as the initial parent score. 

B.2 Trajectory Collection and Label Construction 

SFT/evaluation pool: two-stage collection. We construct the SFT/evaluation pool using a two-stage procedure. In the first stage, we run ten sequential evolution steps on each selected task, starting from its initial executable solution. The solution produced at each step is retained as a parent for the second stage. In the second stage, we independently propose and execute 20 child solutions for each retained parent, with all 20 children evaluated against the same parent. Gemini-3.1-Pro generates the ideas and implements the solutions in both stages. The resulting parent–child pairs form the candidate pool for SFT and static evaluation. 

Parent-specific labels. For each parent p, we compute the signed relative improvements of its executed children, orienting the task metric so that positive values indicate better performance than the parent. We exclude infeasible child implementations and parents with fewer than three improving children. The child outcomes associated with each retained parent provide the empirical mean μp and standard deviation σp used to construct the five-level labels. 

GRPO pool: improvement-driven collection. We collect GRPO training examples using a separate iterative procedure across the designated training tasks. At each step, Gemini-3.1-Pro proposes and implements one child solution from the current parent. If empirical evaluation shows an improvement, the child becomes the parent for the next step; otherwise, the current parent is retained. This procedure collects examples across a changing set of parents as successful modifications accumulate. The initial GRPO pool contains approximately 25K execution-labeled examples, with an imbalanced distribu-tion of Improve , Tie , and Lose outcomes. We randomly downsample within outcome classes to obtain a balanced training set of 15,669 examples, comprising 5,223 examples per class, as imbalanced distribution will cause critic model GRPO training to always output the majority label. This pool is used for critic GRPO training and is separate from the SFT/evaluation pool.

For each parent p, we generate and execute 20 child solutions and compute their signed relative improvements ∆p, 1, . . . , ∆p, 20 , orienting each task metric so that a positive value always indicates improvement. We filter out parents that have less than 3 improved children solutions. # H OpenEvolve Evaluation Details 

H.1 OpenEvolve Framework 

Evolutionary search. OpenEvolve (Sharma, 2025) is an evolutionary coding framework for recursive self-improvement. It begins with an executable seed program and maintains a population of previously evaluated solutions. At each evolutionary round, OpenEvolve samples a parent solution, together with additional high-performing or diverse solutions that serve as inspirations, and prompts a language model to propose a modification. The resulting child program is executed by a task-specific evaluator, which returns empirical performance metrics and execution feedback. The evaluated child is then added to the population and can be selected as a parent or inspiration in subsequent rounds. Repeating this process allows improvements discovered in earlier rounds to be accumulated into progressively stronger solutions. 

Population and island management. OpenEvolve combines a quality-diversity archive based on MAP-Elites with an island-based population structure. MAP-Elites partitions candidate solutions according to configurable behavioral or structural features and retains a high-performing solution within each occupied region, preserving diverse approaches rather than optimizing only a single lineage. Multiple islands maintain semi-independent populations and therefore explore different evolutionary trajectories. High-performing solutions are periodically migrated between islands, allowing useful discoveries from one population to influence the others while reducing premature convergence. Parent selection balances exploitation of high-performing solutions with exploration of diverse or less frequently sampled regions of the population. OpenEvolve also tracks the best verified solution separately so that it is preserved throughout evolution. 

Configuration in our experiments. For each task and screening setting, we maintain three islands and evolve each island for 10 rounds. Exactly one selected idea is implemented and empirically verified in each island round, resulting in a total budget of 30 verification runs per task. We set migration interval as 4(migration happens every 4 evolution rounds). The idea proposer is using temperature of 0.8, and idea and implementation summarizer is using temperature of 0.4. All critic models are using temperature of 0.7. All settings begin from the same seed implementation and use the same proposer, implementer, summarizer, population configuration, and verification budget; they differ only in whether and how candidate ideas are screened before execution.

MediumWin > SmallWin > Tie > Lose and calculate an average rank for each candidate idea. Only the candidate idea with the highest average rank for execution.
